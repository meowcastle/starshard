# Pilot Search and Fidelity: Code's Five Questions

Research doc, 12 Oct 2026 · live copy: https://claude.ai/code/artifact/c562f5cb-eb9e-40f8-abae-c63e4b2d5d24

## The short answers

**For:** the Code session, in reply to `NOTE-TO-RESEARCH-12OCT.md` · **Status:** research and recommendations; nothing built.

1. **Search cost.** Search less, not cheaper. Gate the search on the evaluator's own uncertainty (only when two or more candidates sit within a band of the static score), race the play-outs instead of fixing a count (sequential halving drops losers after 2–3 play-outs each), and shorten the horizon from four turns to "until my next turn" with Forge's `GameStateEvaluator` at the leaf, which already predicts combat. The MTG and Hearthstone literature got strong play from 0.2–1 s per decision this way; the 5-minute game is a budget problem, not a search problem. Target: under 20 s a game, within 10x of stock.
2. **Strength and fidelity.** They are different targets and the chess evidence says so directly: Maia, a model trained on humans at a rating level, predicts those humans' moves at 46–52%, while Stockfish, far stronger, manages 33–41%, and weakening Stockfish makes it worse, not better ([McIlroy-Young et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.01855v1)). A strong pilot will fix the matchups where Forge's error is *systematic* (instant-based decks played as if they had no instants), because any competent pilot and any pro agree on those plays. It will not make Forge's *level* match an RCQ field, and if the pilot ever played at team level it would overshoot the real data by the same 5–10 points the pros beat that field by. Measure fidelity per archetype class, not just k: the test is whether control decks' betas shrink while aggro decks' do not grow.
3. **Which population.** Calibrate against the population the sim's users play in, and that is paper RCQ and store championship fields plus Arena Bo3, not MTGO Challenges. But fit on everything with weights, and let the data decide the weights: fit k and the betas on paper-only and on MTGO-only and compare. If the two fits agree within noise, pool; if MTGO betas are systematically closer to Forge's (plausible, since Challenge pilots are stronger and more deck-disciplined), weight MTGO down for the headline and up for the archetype-strength prior.
4. **Combat and role.** Nobody has measured how often role misassignment decides a Standard game, and I could not find a dataset that would allow it. We can measure it ourselves in a day: Forge's evaluator gives a score every turn, so count games whose lead changed hands during a combat step, and games where the loser had the higher static score the turn before its last attack or block. That number, per archetype pairing, is the priority list for where search should run.
5. **Dead cards at scale.** Run the finder per card, not per deck: for every Standard-legal card (Scryfall `f:standard`, roughly 4,000–5,000 unique cards), four copies in a 24-land two-colour shell, three games against a stock aggro list, flag cards never cast and cards cast with no effect. Three to six hours of NAS time once, then a few minutes per new set, and the output is a `dead.json` the deck check reads before any run. Add Forge's own `AI:RemoveDeck` scan and its game-1 warning as two more columns so the three sources can be compared.

## Q1. Making play-out search affordable

The current searcher costs about 5 minutes a game because it searches every main-phase decision with a real choice, runs a fixed number of four-turn play-outs per candidate, and pays a full `GameCopier` copy for each. Three things cut that by an order of magnitude each, and they stack.

**What the literature did with a similar engine.** The one peer-reviewed MCTS for Magic, Cowling, Ward and Powley's ensemble-determinization work, searched *only the card-play decision in the main phase*, left attacks and blocks to rules, and ran 10,000 simulations per decision split over 40 determinized trees in about 0.75 s; with move pruning and a binary-tree formulation that fell to about 0.2 s per decision while beating a strong expert-rules AI 50.5% of the time ([Cowling, Ward and Powley, 2012](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/75050/1/EnsDetMagic.pdf)). Their rollouts went to the end of the game with a cheap rules policy, and the single biggest speed-up was shrinking the branching factor (card-by-card decisions instead of subsets). In Hearthstone, Świechowski, Tajmajer and Janusz replaced full random rollouts with a learned win-probability network that cuts play-outs off after about 20 steps and found it "substantially improve[s] the win rate" while reducing computation: 78–82% against plain MCTS at 0.5 s a move, from a simulator running about 10,000 full games a second ([Improving Hearthstone AI](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1808.04794)). The Hearthstone AI Competition allowed 60 s a turn and its strong entries were one-turn lookahead with a heuristic evaluator, not deep search ([Dockhorn and Mostaghim](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1906.04238)). The pattern across both games: search the main-phase card choice, stop early, score with a heuristic, keep the branching factor small.

**What Forge already gives us.** `GameStateEvaluator` is a static scorer that, before scoring, copies the game and advances to combat damage when the active player has creatures, so a one-ply evaluation already includes the predicted combat ([GameStateEvaluator.java](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Card-Forge/forge/master/forge-ai/src/main/java/forge/ai/simulation/GameStateEvaluator.java)). Its known blind spots are listed in its own comments: it does not value holding counterspell mana, does not score creatures relative to the opponent's board, and ignores the opponent's mana base. `SpellAbilityPicker` enumerates castable abilities, dedupes identical cards, and only plays a candidate whose simulated score beats the current one; it holds instants until main 1 so they can be compared with sorcery-speed plays ([SpellAbilityPicker.java](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Card-Forge/forge/master/forge-ai/src/main/java/forge/ai/simulation/SpellAbilityPicker.java)). `GameSimulator` copies the whole game per candidate, resolves the stack for the weakest opponent, and has a TODO that pre-blocker combat is not simulated ([GameSimulator.java](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Card-Forge/forge/master/forge-ai/src/main/java/forge/ai/simulation/GameSimulator.java)). The hybrid mode merged in July 2026 is this picker at depth zero as a veto on the heuristic's choice; the full simulation AI keeps depth three ([PR #11436](https://github.com/Card-Forge/forge/pull/11436)). Code measured both: no gain from hybrid, and full simulation 15x slower with processes dying. That is consistent with a static evaluator being too weak to search *deep*, while being fine as a leaf for a *shallow* search over play-outs, which is what the Hearthstone result says too.

**Three cuts, in order of payoff.**

| Cut | What to do | Expected saving | How to check it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Search only uncertain decisions | Score every legal candidate once with `GameStateEvaluator` (one copy each, no play-out). If the best static score beats the second by more than a margin m (start at the evaluator's own one-creature value, roughly 100–150 points), take Forge's heuristic choice and skip the play-outs. Search only when two or more candidates sit inside m. Never search land drops, mana abilities, or turns where only one spell is castable. | Most turns have one obvious play; expect 60–80% of decisions to skip the play-outs. | Log the share of decisions searched and the win rate vs stock at m = 0, 100, 300. The curve should be flat until m is large. |
| 2. Race the play-outs | Replace the fixed count with sequential halving: give every surviving candidate 2 play-outs, drop the bottom half, double the play-outs for the rest, repeat until one is left or the budget is spent ([Karnin, Koren and Somekh, 2013](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v28/karnin13)); or the simpler Hoeffding race, which drops a candidate as soon as its confidence interval falls below the leader's ([Maron and Moore, 1993](https://papers.neurips.cc/paper/1993/hash/02a32ad2669e6fe298e607fe7cc0e1a0-Abstract.html)). Keep the current "Forge's choice stands unless beaten by 2 SE" rule as the final gate. | With 4 candidates and a 16-play-out budget, halving spends 16 where a fixed scheme spends 32–64; the saving grows with the candidate count. | Same win rate vs stock at half the play-outs. |
| 3. Shorten the horizon and cheapen the leaf | Play out to the end of the opponent's next turn (one full turn cycle, two at most), then score the leaf with `GameStateEvaluator`, which already predicts the next combat. Four turns of two heuristic AIs is mostly noise: the random opponent hand re-deal and the AI's own randomness swamp the signal of the move being tested, which is why the honest run needs so many play-outs. | Roughly 2–3x per play-out from the shorter horizon; the copy cost stays, so budget the number of play-outs, not the horizon, if memory is the limit. | Win rate vs stock at horizon 1, 2, 4 on the same seeds. |

**Which decisions are worth searching, by expected gain.** From the accuracy and Standard research, the decisions where Forge's heuristic is weakest and a one-turn play-out can see the difference: (a) main-phase spells when the opponent has open mana (cast into a counter, or bait first); (b) whether to hold up an instant or tap out, which the evaluator cannot see but a one-cycle play-out can, since the opponent's turn is inside the horizon; (c) attacks where the race is close (role assignment, Q4), which the current searcher leaves to Forge. Decisions not worth searching: land drops, mulligans (rules are cheaper and better), targeting among near-identical targets, anything in a turn where the static scores are separated by more than m.

**Determinization honesty.** The leaky run (copies could see the opponent's real hand) gave 59%; the honest run re-deals the opponent's unseen cards. Cowling et al. used exactly that scheme and named its two failure modes, strategy fusion and non-locality, and still found it competitive at 20–100 determinizations per decision. The cheap version for us: re-deal once per play-out (not once per decision), so the ensemble over play-outs is the ensemble over worlds; and bias the re-deal toward the archetype's real list (we know the 60, not the hand), which is strictly more information than Cowling's agents had.

**Budget arithmetic.** Stock: 6 s median game, roughly 10–15 decisions with a real choice. If cut 1 leaves 3–4 searched decisions, cut 2 holds them to about 12 play-outs each, and cut 3 makes a play-out about 0.3–0.5 s, the search adds 10–25 s a game: 3–5x stock, not 30–60x. That is cheap enough to run the pilot on both sides in production for the pairings that are flagged tainted today, and for calibration round robins overnight.

**A cheaper evaluator than play-outs.** The Hearthstone result says a learned value function trained on the engine's own games beats rollouts. We have the data to train one: every production game log has the evaluator's score per turn and the result. A gradient-boosted model on the evaluator's features plus a few we know it lacks (open mana on the opponent's side, cards in hand by type, whether the deck is reactive) predicting win probability from a mid-game state would give a leaf score in microseconds and could replace cut 3's play-outs entirely for the uncertain decisions. That is a week's work after the three cuts; do it only if the cuts leave search still too slow for production.

## Q2. Does pilot strength translate to fidelity?

Partly, and the part it does not cover is the one to watch. Strength removes *systematic* error; fidelity to a population also needs the right *level* of error, and a pilot can be too good for the field it is calibrated against.

**The chess evidence.** Maia is AlphaZero's architecture trained on human games at nine rating bands. Each Maia model predicts human moves best for players near the rating it was trained on, at 46–52% move-matching; Stockfish, vastly stronger than any of those humans, matches 33–41%, and weakening Stockfish to human strength lowers its match rate further; Leela's stronger nets do best among engines at 46% but barely vary across human ratings, so they say nothing about how a 1300 plays differently from an 1900 ([McIlroy-Young, Sadler, Anderson and Kleinberg, 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.01855v1); [summary](https://blog.acolyer.org/2020/09/14/aligning-superhuman-ai-with-human-behaviour/)). A 2024 follow-up adds the other half: adding a little search to a human-trained policy left move prediction almost unchanged (55.7% to 55.9%) but raised playing strength by about 400 Elo against strong opponents ([Human-aligned Chess with a Bit of Search](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.03893)). Strength and human-likeness are separable, and search buys the first without the second.

**What that means for Forge.** Today's error is of the Stockfish-weakened kind in reverse: Forge is not weak the way RCQ players are weak. It is weak in a way no human is (never holds up a counter, casts 3–9 spells a game with 6–9 lands, keeps on land count alone). Fixing those moves the pilot *toward* every human population at once, which is why the reactive pilot both strengthened Azorius Control (13% to 27%) and moved it toward its real 41%. Expect that to continue until the systematic errors are gone. After that, further strength is Leela: it raises self-play win rate and flattens the differences between decks that the real field, with its own mistakes, actually shows. Signs of overshoot to watch for: aggro decks' real-vs-Forge gap opening the other way (a pilot that never misblocks makes aggro worse than RCQ fields find it), and the control mirror or combo pairings becoming *more* lopsided than the data.

**Where the ceiling sits.** The pros beat RCQ-level fields by 5–10 points (Standard research: team members 61–66% against Pro Tour fields, which are themselves stronger than RCQ fields). A pilot that reached that level on both sides would be modelling Pro Tour Standard, where the archetype spread is 40–70%, not store Standard, where mistakes compress it. k is fitted on store and Challenge data, so k would plateau or fall even as self-play strength kept rising. That is not a reason to stop improving the pilot; it is a reason to keep two numbers: self-play strength (for the pilot) and k per population (for the calibration), and to accept that the calibration layer, not the pilot, carries the level.

**The test Code can run now.** Three pilots on both sides of the same 20-deck round robin, same seeds: stock Forge; reactive pilot for the decks that pass the mirror gate; reactive pilot everywhere. Refit calibration on each and report, per archetype class: k, the mean absolute beta, and the sign of the betas. Prediction: control-class betas shrink across the three (less overrating of the field against control), aggro-class betas stay flat, and k rises from 0.07 toward 0.15–0.2 then stops. If aggro betas flip sign, that is overshoot and the pilot should be held at the previous setting for calibration runs while remaining available for "best play" runs.

**Two products, one engine.** The sim can honestly offer both: "how these lists do when both sides are piloted well" (pilot at full strength, no calibration, the pros' own question) and "how these lists do in your field" (pilot at the level that minimises k's residuals, calibration on). Name them on the page; mixing them is how 57% gets read as a forecast.

## Q3. Which real-data population to calibrate against

The population the users play in. That is RCQs, store championships and Arena Bo3 ladders for Justin and friends, not MTGO Challenges, and it is the paper data the calibration started with. MTGO is still worth keeping, because it is the bigger and cleaner sample; the question is weight, not inclusion.

**How the populations differ.** Pro Tour fields show archetype spreads of 40–70% with the best teams at 60–68%; Karsten's own caution is that at a Pro Tour "it's impossible to disentangle excellence of the players and their decks" ([Karsten](https://magic.gg/news/metagame-mentor-the-highest-standard-win-rates-at-pro-tour-march-of-the)). MTGO Challenge fields are the next tier down: self-selected, deck-disciplined grinders, lists one week fresher than paper. Store and RCQ fields are wider in skill and in list quality. Modern Nexus's MTGO study refused to mix game-1 and post-board rates for the same reason the populations should not be pooled blindly: they differ by matchup, not just by level ([Modern Nexus](https://modernnexus.com/matchups-and-win-rates-top-tier-decks-part-2/)). The 17lands experience is the same lesson from Limited: the ranked Bo1 queue compresses the top-to-bottom gap by about a point against the unranked Bo3 queue, so the same decks carry different numbers in different populations.

**What to do, in order.**

1. **Tag every real match with its level** (already partly in `realdata.js` SOURCES): Pro Tour / Regional Championship, MTGO Challenge, RCQ, store championship, league, Arena if ever added.
2. **Fit three ways and compare:** paper-only, MTGO-only, pooled with the work order's weights (PT/RC x2, Challenge x1.5, RCQ x1, store x0.75). Report k and the per-archetype betas for each. If paper and MTGO betas agree within their ranges, pool and move on. If they disagree in a pattern (MTGO betas smaller for control decks, say), the populations really differ and the sim should carry a population switch.
3. **Default the headline to the users' population** (paper RCQ/store), and use MTGO as the prior for archetype strength where paper data is thin: the empirical-Bayes pull already exists; give it a population-aware centre.
4. **Do not weight by level alone.** Level weighting assumes higher-level data is *truer*; for this product it is *different*. Weight by sample quality (draws excluded, archetype label confidence) and recency instead, and treat level as a covariate: one extra term per level in the log-odds fit, which the current beta-per-archetype model can absorb as `beta_archetype + gamma_level`.
5. **Recency over volume.** Real games from before the last set release or ban are a different format. A half-life of 4–6 weeks on the real-data weight matches how fast the Regional Championship round-ups show the deck to beat changing ([Karsten, six RCs](https://magic.gg/news/metagame-mentor-standard-developments-at-six-regional-championships-and-magic-spotlight-secrets)).

**Acceptance.** k's 95% range narrows, and the paper-only and pooled headline numbers for the current top-8 field differ by less than the range. If they do not, the page shows the paper number and names the population.

## Q4. Combat and role assignment

No one has measured how often role misassignment decides a Standard game. The claim rests on expert testimony: Flores's "Misassignment of role equals game loss" ([Who's the Beatdown?](https://articles.starcitygames.com/articles/whos-the-beatdown/)), Duke's "failing to ask at all will most certainly lead to a quick and convincing loss," and Klomparens's method of testing deliberately aggressive and deliberately defensive postures in midrange mirrors to find when to "turn the corner," which she says should be "as soon as you possibly can" ([Matchup Analysis](https://articles.starcitygames.com/articles/a-practical-guide-to-matchup-analysis/)). The nearest data is indirect: in Limited, top players' biggest per-card edges are on decision-heavy cards, and the pick-quality gap explains only half the skill gap, leaving 5–10 points for play, of which combat is the largest in-game component ([17lands, Learning from the Best](https://blog.17lands.com/posts/learning-from-the-best/)).

**Measure it in our own logs.** Forge's evaluator gives a score for each side every turn, and the full game log records every attack and block. Three counts per archetype pairing, computable from existing production logs in a day:

| Count | Definition | What it tells us |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Combat lead changes | Games where the sign of (my score minus theirs) flipped during a combat step and never flipped back | How often combat, rather than a spell, decided the game |
| Loser-ahead-before-combat | Games where the loser had the higher static score on the turn before its last attack or its last block | How often the deciding combat was a misjudged attack or block, the role-assignment signature |
| Race crossings | Games where both players' projected turns-to-kill (life divided by on-board damage) crossed, i.e. the beatdown changed hands | How often the role actually switched mid-game, which is when a fixed posture is wrong |

Forge sets each AI's posture once from the deck name or average mana value and keeps it all game ([PlayerControllerAi](https://github.com/Card-Forge/forge/blob/master/forge-ai/src/main/java/forge/ai/PlayerControllerAi.java)); the pros say the role is per matchup and per turn. The third count measures exactly the gap between those two.

**What to do with the numbers.** Rank pairings by loser-ahead-before-combat. The top of that list is where a combat search pays, and it is the answer to "which decisions are worth searching" for attacks and blocks: run the play-out search on declare-attackers only in those pairings, and only when the race-crossing test says the roles are close (projected turns-to-kill within one turn of each other). Everywhere else, Forge's `AiAttackController` stays. The rule-set version is cheaper still and should be tried first: set the posture per matchup from the two lists (the deck with the shorter projected clock is the beatdown), re-evaluate it each turn from the board, and veto attacks that the evaluator's combat prediction scores as losing. Code already has the veto half from the hybrid work.

**Acceptance.** On the five pairings with the highest loser-ahead rate, the posture rule or the combat search raises the piloted deck's mirror win rate above 55% against stock (the existing mirror gate), and the loser-ahead rate itself falls. If the rate does not fall, the deciding combats were forced, not misjudged, and the search should move elsewhere.

## Q5. Dead cards at scale

The deck finder found three dead cards the scripts do not flag in the 20 meta decks alone, and release weeks are when the sim is asked about cards nobody has tested. A per-card harness answers it once per set.

**The harness.**

1. **Card list.** Scryfall `f:standard -is:digital` for unique card names (Standard holds 14–19 sets plus Foundations, so roughly 4,000–5,000 names; the exact count comes from the query). Skip basic lands. Keep the oracle id so reprints collapse.
2. **Shell per card.** Four copies of the card in a 60-card shell: 24 lands matching the card's colours (two-colour shells from Forge's basic and dual pool), and 32 filler slots from a fixed set of simple creatures and removal in those colours, chosen once per colour pair and known to be AI-playable. The filler exists only so games end; the test is whether the card ever gets cast.
3. **Games.** Three games against a stock mono-red list with the AI on both sides, `-q`, 180 s clock, seeds fixed per card so the run is reproducible. Mono-red ends games fast and makes every turn a real board, so a card that is castable gets its window. Cards that are only castable under conditions the shell cannot create (cards that need artifacts, a graveyard full of creatures, a commander) will false-flag; keep a short allow-list and treat their flag as "conditional" rather than dead.
4. **Parse.** From the full log, per card: times cast, times drawn (the AI's hand is in the log), and the game-1 "AI can't play these cards well" warning. Dead = drawn 3 or more times across the games and never cast. Limited = cast but `AI:RemoveDeck:Random` or warned. Also record Forge's `AI:RemoveDeck:All` from the card script (Code's existing scan), so the three sources sit side by side.
5. **Output.** `data/dead.json`: `{oracleId, name, status: ok|conditional|limited|dead, source: [script|warning|harness], set, checkedAt}`. The deck check reads it before any run; the tainted flag uses it; the coach names the cards.

**Cost.** 5,000 cards x 3 games x roughly 6 s = about 25 hours of single-core game time; the NAS and the Mac worker between them run 4–8 games in parallel, so 3–6 hours once. A new set is 250–300 cards: 15–30 minutes. Run it the night a set hits Forge's snapshot, which is before the Regional Championship weekend that follows every release.

**What it will find.** Expect three classes, from the deck finder's first results: cards whose effect has no AI logic (Jace's Machinations), cards whose extra or alternative cost the AI cannot evaluate (Countersculpt's "behold"), and cards the AI could cast but never chooses to (Appa, a flash creature). The first two are Forge bugs worth reporting upstream with the harness output as the repro; the third is a pilot problem the narrow dead-card pilot already handles. Separating them in `dead.json` (`reason: nologic|cost|never-chosen`) tells Code which fix applies.

**Acceptance.** The harness reproduces the three known dead cards and flags nothing in a control set of 50 cards from the top-8 decks that the deck finder passed. Then the first full run's dead list, sorted by how many real decklists in the Jiliac cache play each card: that sorted list is the release-week risk report.

## Recommended order of work

Each item is a day or two except the harness run itself, and each has a number that says whether it worked.

| # | Item | Measure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Finish the honest Monte Carlo run already in progress; record win rate vs stock and seconds per game as the baseline | Baseline only |
| 2 | Q1 cut 1, search only uncertain decisions (static-score margin m) | Share of decisions searched; win rate vs stock flat from m = 0 to m = 100 |
| 3 | Q1 cut 2, sequential halving over play-outs | Same win rate at half the play-outs |
| 4 | Q1 cut 3, one-cycle horizon with the evaluator at the leaf | Win rate vs stock at horizon 1 vs 4; seconds per game under 20 |
| 5 | Q4 log counts (lead changes, loser-ahead, race crossings) over existing production logs | A ranked list of pairings; no engine change |
| 6 | Q4 posture rule per matchup with the hybrid veto, on the top five pairings from item 5 | Mirror gate passed (55%+); loser-ahead rate falls |
| 7 | Q2 three-pilot calibration test | k and per-class betas for stock / gated / everywhere; control betas shrink, aggro betas do not flip |
| 8 | Q3 three-way fit (paper, MTGO, pooled) with level as a covariate and a 4–6 week half-life | k range narrows; headline differs by less than the range across fits |
| 9 | Q5 per-card harness, `dead.json`, deck check reads it | Reproduces the three known dead cards; first release-week risk report |
| 10 | Learned leaf evaluator from production logs, only if item 4 leaves search too slow | Win rate vs stock with the model at the leaf vs play-outs, at a tenth of the time |

Items 2–4 are independent of 5–9 and can run in parallel with them. The page should not show piloted numbers as the headline until item 7 has reported.
