# What Makes a Great Limited Player

Research doc, 12 Oct 2026 · live copy: https://claude.ai/code/artifact/98b9e548-4d2d-4f6a-83c6-85bd9e301ec2

## The answer

The best Limited players win about 60% of their games where an average player wins 50%, and the data says most of that gap is not "they take better cards." When 17lands scored top players' decks by the same card ratings everyone uses, the better card choices were worth about +0.4 points of win rate ([17lands, Learning from the Best](https://blog.17lands.com/posts/learning-from-the-best/)). The other ten points come from three things the card ratings can't see: knowing *when* a card is good (the deck and the lane it ends up in), building a deck that functions every time, and playing the games.

So the technique-vs-deck-building split is the wrong cut. The cut the experts actually make is:

1.  **Drafting sets the floor.** Ben Stark's claim, in the article pros still call the most important one written on drafting: draft "the hard way" (read what's open, drop early picks) and "you can have a good deck in 90% of your drafts"; draft the easy way and about half your decks are trainwrecks, and "when you have trainwrecks it is very difficult to beat anyone" ([Drafting the Hard Way](https://benstark398565291.wordpress.com/2020/06/13/example-post-3/)). Pick quality alone is worth 6–7 points between the best and worst drafters ([Sierkovitz, Dec 2024](https://threadreaderapp.com/thread/1864654641151041894.html)).

2.  **Play decides between functional decks.** Stark again: with a merely functional deck against the best deck in the pod, at equal play skill, "I win that match around 45% of the time." Márcio Carvalho, three-time Pro Tour finalist: "If I play better than my opponent in Limited, I think I have a 20 percent edge, maybe more" ([Dot Esports](https://dotesports.com/mtg/news/limited-exposure-as-magic-arena-grows-competitive-limited-takes-a-backseat)).

3.  **Luck is most of any single game.** A model with the deck, the pilot's skill tier, play/draw and mulligans explains about a quarter of game outcomes ([mtgds, 2022](https://mtgds.wordpress.com/2022/02/28/knowledge-and-power-estimating-adjusted-win-rate-in-magic-the-gathering-limited/)); a transformer trained on 400,000 drafts hits a ceiling of ρ ≈ 0.23 predicting a draft's result from its picks ([Rigaux & Kashima, 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.04782)). The skill shows over hundreds of games, never in one.

If you want one sentence from the experts: fundamentals (curve, two-drops, creatures, cheap interaction, two colours) beat cleverness, staying open beats committing, and review beats volume. Where they disagree is where to spend your hours first: Sierkovitz says card evaluation, because those gaps are "much easier to bridge"; Carvalho and the coaching crowd say play, because among good drafters it decides.

## What the data says

The gap between the best and worst drafters on 17lands is 10–12 points of game win rate, and it is the same size in every colour pair, which is the first clue that it is mostly the pilot, not the deck. Numbers pulled from 17lands' own data endpoints on 12 Oct 2026 unless noted.

| Format                                      | Everyone            | Top tier | Bottom tier | Gap  |
|---------------------------------------------|---------------------|----------|-------------|------|
| Edge of Eternities, Premier Draft (Bo1)     | 54.8% (1.14M games) | 59.4%    | 48.0%       | 11.4 |
| Edge of Eternities, Traditional Draft (Bo3) | 59.6% (196k)        | 65.3%    | 52.9%       | 12.4 |
| Final Fantasy, Premier Draft (Bo1)          | 55.1% (1.42M)       | 59.4%    | 49.4%       | 10.0 |

Source: [17lands colour ratings data](https://www.17lands.com/color_ratings/data?expansion=EOE&event_type=PremierDraft&start_date=2025-07-29&end_date=2025-10-12&combine_splash=true) with `user_group=top|bottom`. Two cautions: 17lands users are better than average (their 55% in Bo1 is against the whole Arena population), and Bo1 is rank-matched, which squeezes the gap; the unranked Bo3 gap is the truer one.

What the gap is made of, as far as anyone has measured it:

- **Better-rated cards: about 0.4 points.** 17lands scored every deck by its cards' all-player win rates; top players' decks scored barely higher. Their real edges were on context cards: Goldhound won 8.5 points more for top players, who also played it less often ([Learning from the Best, 2022](https://blog.17lands.com/posts/learning-from-the-best/)).

- **Pick quality: about 6–7 points, overlapping with skill.** The chart below. Drafters took the pack's best card first-pick only 30% of the time, and the win rate tracked their average pick rank, not how many of those picks made the deck.

*Chart in the live doc: Bo1 game win rate by average pick rank (cards ranked by win rate within each pack), about 64,000 drafts; the 17lands average is 55%.*

| Average rank of the card taken (1 = best in pack) | Game win rate |
| --- | --- |
| 1–2 | 57.5% |
| 3 | 54.5% |
| 4 | 53.5% |
| 5+ | 51.0% |

Source: Sierkovitz (17lands analyst), thread of 5 Dec 2024, first eight picks of each pack; rank buckets rounded from the thread's ranges

- **Archetype choice: about 3 points.** Best colour pair to worst in Edge of Eternities was 56.3% to 53.2%, and the same 3-point spread held inside the top tier (60.6% to 57.5%). Going to three colours cost 3.6 points in Final Fantasy (55.6% two-colour vs 52.0%), five colours cost 10.

- **Play/draw: 2–7 points depending on the set.** On-the-play win rate in Premier Draft runs from 50.1% (Dominaria) to 53.7% (Modern Horizons 3), with recent sets at 53%; Cube is 55.7% ([17lands play/draw table](https://www.17lands.com/data/play_draw)). The draw of a turn-one mana creature in Foundations: 66% on the play vs 54% without it ([Sierkovitz](https://threadreaderapp.com/user/Sierkovitz)).

- **Mulligans: about 14 points each.** In a per-game model on Crimson Vow data, each own mulligan cost 14.4 points and each opponent mulligan gave 13.9; being on the play was worth 4.0 ([mtgds](https://mtgds.wordpress.com/2022/02/28/knowledge-and-power-estimating-adjusted-win-rate-in-magic-the-gathering-limited/)).

- **Everything measurable, together: about a quarter of the outcome.** That model's predictions correlated 0.24 with results. The 2026 Kyoto paper on deck strength finds the same ceiling (ρ ≈ 0.23) from the other direction ([arXiv 2607.04782](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.04782)). A good drafter at 60% trophies about one Premier Draft in four; a 50% player one in eleven (my arithmetic on the 7-win structure).

For scale: in the economics literature that measures skill against chance, a one-standard-deviation-better player beats an average one 72.9% of the time in chess, 54.2% in Skat and 53.3% in heads-up poker ([Duersch, Lambrecht & Oechssler, 2020](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/chess/2020-duersch.pdf)). A 53%-to-65% tier gap in unranked Bo3 puts Limited well above poker per game and far below chess, which matches how pros talk about it: real skill, slow to show.

## Drafting and building: what the best drafters say they do

The experts converge on one idea with different words: you are drafting a deck that functions, not a pile of the best cards, and the skill is reading what the table is giving you.

**Stay open and read the table.** Ben Stark: "The single thing people do that ruins most drafts is stick to their early picks"; drafting the hard way means "always being willing to drop early picks if you realize something else is open" and analysing each new format for what matters in it, since "different things are a little more or a little less important in each new format" ([Drafting the Hard Way](https://benstark398565291.wordpress.com/2020/06/13/example-post-3/)). Paulo Vitor Damo da Rosa: "I'm someone who tries to stay open as much as possible during the draft" and "we also need to pay attention to what we're sending our neighbours" ([Drafting the Correct Way](https://www.threeforonetrading.com/en/drafting-the-correct-way)). Reid Duke adds the counterweight: "a signal will never prove anything beyond a doubt" and "the more you switch colours, the more picks you waste" ([Level One: Signals](https://magic.wizards.com/en/articles/archive/level-one/signals-booster-draft-2015-01-19)). Ryan Saxe says the skill most people skip is watching the wheel: "if you want to get better at drafting, you need to pay more attention in the later picks of the pack" ([Understanding the Wheel](https://articles.starcitygames.com/articles/understanding-the-wheel-while-drafting/)).

**Deck, not cards.** Sam Black: "drafting isn't just about drafting the best cards; it's about drafting a deck" and "you get 42 picks, but you probably only play about 24 cards," so count your playables as you go ([Draft the Good Cards](https://articles.starcitygames.com/articles/draft-the-good-cards/)). Stark: "You don't need to be in the best colour or archetype... if your deck is fully functional, meaning the cards you do have work together" you are live. Reid Duke keeps the bomb exception: "Synergy is good, but it should not come at the expense of what's most important" and "a bomb is a bomb" ([Level One: Basics of Booster Draft](https://magic.wizards.com/en/articles/archive/level-one/basics-booster-draft-2015-08-03)). Sierkovitz's data-driven version: the fastest improvement is not playing bad cards, before learning to play good ones, because "the gaps in card evaluation are much easier to bridge" than gameplay gaps ([MTG Arena Zone](https://mtgazone.com/how-to-improve-your-mtg-draft-skills-using-data/)).

**Fundamentals: curve, creatures, interaction, two colours.** Marshall Sutcliffe's CABS theory (Cards that Affect the Board State): "Creatures are easily the most important card type in Limited"; "removal spells are the scarcest"; prefer "solid doubles over strikeout/home-run cards" ([CABS Theory](https://magic.wizards.com/en/articles/archive/limited-information/cabs-theory-2015-08-19)). On a 2026 Limited Resources episode with Paul Cheon: "The best Limited players prefer to draft a good mana curve with some interaction; nothing beats that" ([LR 839](https://stationlm.com/analysis/37dce601-0471-43fd-b708-cf1355d50cf0)). Stark, relayed by Marshall: most Limited games are won by curving out with one trick or removal spell. Reid Duke: "I'd rather cast my spells a turn late than not cast them at all" ([Tarkir guide](https://ultimateguard.com/en/blog/reid-duke-guide-tarkir-limited-magic-the-gathering)).

**Where they disagree.** BREAD (bombs, removal, evasion, aggro, duds) as a pick order: Limited Resources rejects it outright ([LR 482](https://lrcast.com/limited-resources-482-a-fresh-look-bread-and-why-you-shouldnt-use-it/)); Reid Duke's course still opens with bombs first. Pick orders in general: Saxe argues "there isn't an objective pick order that will work for everybody" and you should draft to your own tendencies ([Maximizing for Your Own Preferences](https://articles.starcitygames.com/articles/limited-fundamentals-maximizing-for-your-own-preferences/)); the LR school wants a universal baseline first. Commit vs stay open: Stark and Saxe lean open, Duke warns about wasted picks, and Ethan Saks says neither is simply right ([How to Stay Open](https://blog.cardsphere.com/how-to-stay-open-in-draft/)). Jon Finkel, asked how to choose colours: "Just pick the best card"; biggest mistake, "not being open to playing the best deck" ([MTG Pro Tutor](https://mtgprotutor.libsyn.com/297-best-of-jon-finkel-on-how-you-can-improve-as-a-magic-player)).

Not found, despite looking: any expert stating a "60% draft, 40% play" split, or the "draft sets the ceiling, play reaches it" line. The closest are Stark's 45% and 90% figures and Carvalho's 20-point edge, quoted above.

## Play technique: the in-game skills the pros name

Once two functional decks meet, the pros name the same half-dozen skills, and the data points at the same place: the cards where top players' edge is biggest are the decision-heavy ones (Tavern Swindler +8.3 points for top players, Goldhound +8.5), not the vanilla ones ([17lands](https://blog.17lands.com/posts/learning-from-the-best/)). In rough order of how often they come up:

1.  **Role assignment.** Mike Flores, 1999, still the most-cited line in Limited strategy: "Misassignment of role equals game loss"; one deck has to be the beatdown and one the control, and "the player who misassigns himself is inevitably the loser" ([Who's the Beatdown?](https://articles.starcitygames.com/articles/whos-the-beatdown/)). Reid Duke: "many players make this decision far too early," and "failing to ask at all will most certainly lead to a quick and convincing loss" ([Level One: Role Assignment](https://magic.wizards.com/en/articles/archive/level-one/role-assignment-2015-01-05)).

2.  **Having a plan.** Marshall Sutcliffe's test: imagine a great player pausing the match to ask "what is your plan?"; if you can't answer, stop and assess the board ([MTG Pro Tutor](https://mtgprotutor.libsyn.com/74-marshall-sutcliffe-pauses-time-and-asks-whats-your-plan)). LR devoted whole episodes to the in-game decision process ([LR 288](https://lrcast.com/?p=1826)).

3.  **Tempo vs card advantage, read turn by turn.** Duke: "The value of tempo changes from one game to the next, and even one turn to the next" ([Level One: Tempo](https://magic.wizards.com/en/articles/archive/level-one/tempo-2015-07-20)). Carvalho's description of what the skill actually is: "In Limited, you're playing on the fly. What cards can you play around? What cards should you play around? What cards did you see in the draft?" ([Dot Esports](https://dotesports.com/mtg/news/limited-exposure-as-magic-arena-grows-competitive-limited-takes-a-backseat)).

4.  **Mulligans.** The single largest measured in-game lever (about 14 points per mulligan), so the keep/ship decision carries more weight than any one attack. Duke's rule: keep two to five lands, "mulligans are very costly" in Limited, rarely keep one-landers. LSV in a slow format: "slow hands are more than fine" ([IPA guide](https://ultimateguard.com/en/blog/ipa-draft-guide-crash-course-in-one-of-the-best-limited-formats-of-all-time)). The lesson is that the right keep depends on the format's speed, which is why it's a skill and not a rule.

5.  **Combat math and playing around tricks.** Andy Ferguson's version for the control seat: "If you automatically lose if you can't counter their bomb, don't tap out" ([SCG](https://articles.starcitygames.com/magic-the-gathering/the-best-practices-for-identifying-your-strengths-and-weaknesses-in-limited/)). Nick Eisel on bluffing: do it more against solid opponents who won't call and less against very good ones who read it; think on the opponent's turn, then attack quickly whether you have the trick or not ([Bluffing in Limited](https://articles.starcitygames.com/articles/limited-lessons-bluffing-in-limited/)).

6.  **Playing to your outs.** Brennan DeCandio: "knowing your outs in any given game and playing to them is perhaps the most difficult thing to master in Magic"; the goal is "putting yourself in the position to get lucky as often as possible" ([SCG](https://articles.starcitygames.com/articles/the-laws-of-variance-and-tilt/)).

7.  **Sideboarding in Bo3.** Mostly unused and therefore a free edge: "many players miss out on value by sideboarding poorly" ([LR 309](https://lrcast.com/limited-resources-309-sideboarding-level-up/)), and in Arena Bo3 two-thirds never sideboard at all. Kuisma's frame for the lost grindy matchup: "it's better to be a 45%-to-55% underdog than a 35%-to-65% one" ([Hareruya](https://article.hareruyamtg.com/article/80996/?lang=en)).

8.  **The clock.** Reid Duke: "Getting used to using the clock to your advantage is important" ([Hareruya, 2018](https://article.hareruyamtg.com/article/article_en_274/?lang=en)). Jake Browne: "The clock is your friend. Use it."

The honest limit on all of this: nobody has published a same-deck, different-pilot study, so the size of the play edge rests on Carvalho's 20 points, Stark's 45%-with-a-worse-deck, and the 10–12-point tier gap minus the 6–7 points that correlate with pick quality. That leaves something like 5–10 points for play among people who draft comparably, which is roughly what the pros say.

## The mental game and how pros practise

The pros describe improvement as a loop, not a volume: play, ask why the winner won, change something, play again. Reid Duke: "More important than the number of hours you play is how much you're actually taking away from those hours," and after each game "ask yourself why the winning player won"; "great players tend to take responsibility for their own mistakes" ([Becoming a Better Player](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/feature/becoming-better-player-2015-09-28)). Jon Finkel: "Be critical of your own plays." Ben Stark, on the losses: "Luck isn't a person. Luck didn't do this to you."

**How a Pro Tour team prepares a Limited format.** Reid Duke's Team TCGplayer report from Pro Tour Final Fantasy (2025): a dozen players in a house for the week before, drafting "from dawn until midnight," with the centrepiece a six-hour "Limited Meeting" discussing "every single colour, archetype, and individual card," which he called the most useful part because "it gave me the tools I needed to navigate some tricky spots" in the real drafts ([Ultimate Guard](https://ultimateguard.com/en/blog/reid-s-pro-tour-final-fantasy-report-as-a-member-of-team-tcgplayer-magic-the-gathering)). Zev Goldhaber-Gordon's Seedcore team: "one or two drafts every day," a shared card-rating sheet, and "stronger Limited players shadowing weaker ones and taking notes on their picks" ([Draftsim interview](https://draftsim.com/mtg-pro-tour-interview/)). Marshall's milestones for the week of a new set: learn the cards, learn the archetypes, then draft every archetype "numerous times"; Limited specialists reach the third, Constructed players usually stop at the second ([magic.gg](https://magic.gg/news/drafting-magic-the-gathering-final-fantasy-at-the-pro-tour)). Andy Ferguson for the rest of us: read the commons and uncommons for what they do, not how strong they are, look at 17lands trophy decks for archetype shapes, and cap pre-reading at an hour ([SCG](https://articles.starcitygames.com/magic-the-gathering/best-practices-for-learning-a-new-limited-environment/)).

**Review tools.** Marshall photographs board states mid-match to review later, and warns that intuition built on faulty logic reinforces mistakes. Saxe reviews what wheeled to "understand regret." Sierkovitz, who sells 90-minute 17lands log reviews, says the data is "a source of questions, not answers" and "a humble servant that lets you improve your draft and gameplay," not a master that dictates picks ([Using Win Rate Data](https://blog.17lands.com/posts/using-win-rate-data/)). Sam Black trusts it over intuition when they conflict: players "discover their own local maxima," and "look at 17lands over your favourite drafter's tier list" ([SCG](https://articles.starcitygames.com/?p=90527)), while cautioning that "no single statistic tells you everything."

**Tilt and variance.** Duke: "It's okay to feel disappointed when you lose. It's a sign that you care"; but "dwelling too long on a loss will make you miserable and stunt your growth," and "blaming bad luck all the time is unhealthy, but the opposite is dangerous as well." Ben Friedman's tournament rule: don't commiserate about luck between rounds, eat, and "set a new goal each round to win that round" rather than a top-8 goal ([SCG](https://articles.starcitygames.com/articles/your-mind-at-a-magic-tournament/)). Michael Rapp on why variance feels worse than it is: "It's easier to remember the times you've drawn poorly than the times you got lucky" ([Card Kingdom](https://blog.cardkingdom.com/mtg-mindset-understanding-variance/)). Jake Browne's short version: "Never draft angry" and "a draft is a small sample size. Forgive yourself." The arithmetic backs him: it takes 400–1,000 games, or 60–150 drafts, to tell a 55% player from a 50% one with any confidence.

**Fatigue.** Goldhaber-Gordon on what a Pro Tour actually tests: "how hard it is to make the right choice again and again for hours on end." Friedman: "being uncomfortable is the root of being distracted."

## What this means for the sim and the coach

The sim is a Constructed tool today, but the findings above transfer, and three of them point at things the coach should say and the pilot should do.

- **Mulligans are the biggest single in-game lever, and Forge's AI mulligans on land count alone.** At about 14 points per mulligan, the keep/ship decision is worth more than any attack. It is also the cheapest decision to take over in the external-pilot plan (Phase 1), and the one the coach can audit after every run: how often did each side mulligan, and did it win those games.

- **The "functional deck" test is what the coach should run before the matchup numbers.** Stark's point is that a non-functional deck loses to everyone, so no matchup percentage means anything until the deck passes the basics: lands and sources for its colours, enough two-drops for its posture, a curve that matches its role. The check-rows step already exists; these are the rows it should carry, with the numbers above as thresholds.

- **Role assignment is a decision the AI never makes.** Forge picks aggro or control posture from the deck name or its average mana value and keeps it for the game. The pros say the role changes by matchup and by turn. When the pilot takes over attacks and blocks (Phase 2), "who is the beatdown here" is the first rule to encode, and it should be decided from the two lists, not from one.

- **Card stats need the 17lands caveats.** Our per-card "win rate when cast" has the same biases Sierkovitz lists for GIH WR: it flatters cards that show up in long games and cards that only get played in the decks built for them. Show the sample size next to each row and mute anything under the threshold, the way 17lands does.

- **Show the noise.** 400–1,000 games to tell 55% from 50% is the same arithmetic the accuracy doc used for Forge. A deck page that says "57%" with no range is teaching the wrong lesson; the pros' own advice to their students is to stop reading single drafts.

- **For the humans using it: review beats volume.** The coach's thread is the right shape. What it should ask after a run is the question Reid Duke asks after a game: why did the winner win, in this matchup, with these cards.
