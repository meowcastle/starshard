# What Makes an Excellent Standard Player

Research doc, 12 Oct 2026 · live copy: https://claude.ai/code/artifact/e574cebb-2b9b-4b89-9ad2-8695dc558531

## The answer

The best constructed players win about 60–66% of their matches against Pro Tour fields, where the average player wins 50%, and the record says that edge comes from preparation at least as much as from in-game play. Hall of Fame constructed win rates: Paulo Vitor Damo da Rosa 65.7% over 725 matches, Shota Yasooka 63.4% over 771, Kai Budde 61.9% over 608, Owen Turtenwald 62.0% over 1,011 ([magic.gg Hall of Fame pages](https://www.magic.gg/news/paulo-vitor-damo-da-rosa-hall-of-fame)). The two teams that have won most Pro Tours since 2023 ran 60.8% (Handshake, 1,425 matches) and 58.2% (ChannelFireball, 1,568) over 2020–24, and against each other they split 42–41 ([Karsten, Teams by the Numbers](https://magic.gg/news/teams-by-the-numbers-handshake-and-channelfireball-ultimate-guard)). Same decks, same testing, same skill tier: a coin flip. The edge is over the field, not over each other.

Where the edge comes from, in the order the evidence supports:

1.  **Deck choice and build are worth more in Standard than in Limited.** At a single Pro Tour the decks with five or more pilots spread from about 40% to 70% match win rate, and the most-played deck usually lands under 50% (Izzet Prowess: 42% of the field and 48.9% at Pro Tour Final Fantasy; 30% of the field and 49.4% at Pro Tour Secrets of Strixhaven) ([Karsten](https://magic.gg/news/metagame-mentor-standard-lessons-from-pro-tour-magic-the-gathering-final-fantasy), [Karsten](https://magic.gg/news/metagame-mentor-standard-win-rates-and-lessons-from-pro-tour-secrets-of-strixhaven)). Picking the deck the field is not ready for is a 10-point swing, and it is decided before round one. Even so, Karsten says of the best team's 67% at Pro Tour March of the Machine that "it's impossible to disentangle excellence of the players and their decks" ([Karsten](https://magic.gg/news/metagame-mentor-the-highest-standard-win-rates-at-pro-tour-march-of-the)).

2.  **Piloting still decides between players holding the same list.** Reid Duke: "How well you pilot your deck of choice is usually more important than which deck you choose" ([Level One: Choosing Your Deck](https://magic.wizards.com/en/articles/archive/level-one/choosing-your-deck-2015-06-08)). Zevin Faust went 7–0 against Izzet Prowess with Azorius Prison, an archetype that won 45.9% overall, and Karsten's lesson was that a player who "knows their deck inside and out" has a real edge ([Karsten](https://magic.gg/news/metagame-mentor-standard-win-rates-and-lessons-from-pro-tour-secrets-of-strixhaven)).

3.  **Sideboarding is the part of play most players skip, and two of every three games in a match are sideboarded.** Brad Nelson: "Sideboarding is the most integral part of Magic for tournament success at the highest levels" ([A Guide to Sideboarding Correctly](https://articles.starcitygames.com/articles/a-guide-to-sideboarding-correctly/)).

4.  **Luck is most of any one match, and skill shows over hundreds.** A 60% player needs 100–250 matches to be told from a 50% one with confidence, a 55% player 400–1,000; Pro Tour archetype win rates carry ±6-point ranges even at 300 matches ([Karsten](https://magic.gg/news/metagame-mentor-the-lessons-and-winners-of-pro-tour-aetherdrift)). Brian Braun-Duin's verdict on Standard: "Variance exists, but far less than everyone believes" ([The Skill in Standard](https://articles.starcitygames.com/articles/the-skill-in-standard/)).

The one-sentence version from the experts: know the metagame before you have an opinion (Damo da Rosa), tune a stock deck rather than brew (Elenbogen), play it until you know it inside and out (Dominguez, Duke), sideboard for the deck they will have after boarding, not the one they had (Nelson), and after every loss write down the mistake, not the bad draw (Duke, via Sam Black). Where they disagree is only on the first step: Damo da Rosa says play the best-positioned deck even if you dislike it; Duke says a deck you enjoy and know beats a slightly better one you don't.

Skill in constructed is real and measurable, but only over hundreds of matches: the best players in the world win about six matches in ten, and in any one match they lose four times in ten.

What separates those bars from the 50% line, as far as anyone has measured it:

- **Deck choice: a 10–30 point spread at every Pro Tour.** Among archetypes with five or more pilots, the Standard rounds of the last five Pro Tours ran from 36.7% (Sultai Reanimator, 31 pilots, Lorwyn Eclipsed) and 25.9% (Dimir Midrange, 9 pilots, Aetherdrift) at the bottom to 70.7% (Izzet Spellementals, 15 pilots, Lorwyn Eclipsed) and 66.7% (Azorius Control, 7 pilots, Aetherdrift) at the top ([Karsten, PT Lorwyn Eclipsed](https://magic.gg/news/metagame-mentor-standard-win-rates-and-lessons-from-pro-tour-lorwyn-eclipsed); [Karsten, PT Aetherdrift](https://www.magic.gg/news/metagame-mentor-the-lessons-and-winners-of-pro-tour-aetherdrift)). Those numbers mix deck and pilot, and the small rows are noisy, but the pattern repeats: the most-played deck lands near 49%, and a well-positioned deck in prepared hands lands 10–20 points above it.

- **Team membership: about 10 points over the field.** Handshake 60.8% and CFB 58.2% over four years of Pro Tour-level play, individual members 61–66% over 100–200 matches each, and 50.6% head to head ([Karsten](https://magic.gg/news/teams-by-the-numbers-handshake-and-channelfireball-ultimate-guard)). The teams share lists and testing, so the 10 points is skill plus preparation; the head-to-head coin flip says neither team has a play-skill edge the other lacks.

- **The all-time best: 62–66% over a career.** Constructed career rates for Damo da Rosa 65.7%, Yasooka 63.4%, Turtenwald 62.0%, Budde 61.9%; Pro Tour-only rates run 2–4 points lower because the field is stronger (Budde 62.8%, Damo da Rosa 62.2%, Turtenwald 59.7%, Yasooka 58.3%) ([Hall of Fame: Budde](https://www.magic.gg/news/kai-budde-hall-of-fame), [Damo da Rosa](https://www.magic.gg/news/paulo-vitor-damo-da-rosa-hall-of-fame), [Turtenwald](https://magic.gg/news/owen-turtenwald-hall-of-fame), [Yasooka](https://www.magic.gg/news/shota-yasooka-hall-of-fame)). Jon Finkel's 16 Pro Tour Top 8s is the record ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jon_Finkel)).

- **Ratings say the same.** Under the old DCI Elo system a 100-point gap meant a 64% expected win rate and 200 points meant 76%; a rating of 2000+ was "Rare skill. Pro Tour regular" ([Getting to Know Your DCI Rating](https://articles.starcitygames.com/articles/getting-to-know-your-dci-rating/)). On Arena, climbing to Mythic takes about a 60–65% win rate and the best meta decks sit at 55–60% ([Draftsim](https://draftsim.com/mtg-arena-win-rate/)).

- **Play/draw and mulligans move single games more than any decision.** The on-the-play edge in constructed is usually put at 52–55% but the public figures are behind trackers' logins; in Limited, where 17lands measures it, each mulligan costs about 14 points of game win rate ([mtgds](https://mtgds.wordpress.com/2022/02/28/knowledge-and-power-estimating-adjusted-win-rate-in-magic-the-gathering-limited/)). No public constructed dataset of the same quality exists; the only published test is six games from 2007 ([Paskins](https://articles.starcitygames.com/articles/feature-article-sixes-and-sevens-mulligans-in-constructed/)).

How many matches it takes to see any of this: a true 55% player reads anywhere from 33% to 77% over 20 matches, a 60% player needs about 100 matches for the 95% range to clear 50% and about 250 to do so reliably, and a 55% player needs 400–1,000 (the binomial arithmetic from the accuracy doc; same for Forge and for humans). Pro Tour archetype rates come with 95% ranges of about ±6 points on 300 matches: Domain Overlords 55.9% was really 50.6–61.2% ([Karsten](https://www.magic.gg/news/metagame-mentor-the-lessons-and-winners-of-pro-tour-aetherdrift)). Testing guides agree: treat fewer than 75 matches with skepticism and prefer 100 or more, and a 60% win rate in MTGO leagues is the benchmark for a deck worth keeping ([Modern Nexus](https://modernnexus.com/systematic-and-practical-testing-guidelines/)). For scale, in games where skill has been measured against chance, a one-standard-deviation-better player beats an average one 72.9% of the time in chess and 53.3% in heads-up poker ([Duersch, Lambrecht and Oechssler, 2020](https://www.gwern.net/doc/psychology/chess/2020-duersch.pdf)); a 60–66% edge for the world's best over a Pro Tour field puts constructed Magic well above poker per match and well below chess.

## Deck selection and building: what the best players say they do

In Standard the deck is chosen, not drafted, so the experts' first skill is reading the field, and their second is resisting the urge to build something new.

**Read the metagame before you have an opinion.** Damo da Rosa's rule from his tournament-preparation article: "you have to have a grasp of the metagame first," and while doing that "Your opinion, while figuring the metagame, is completely irrelevant"; then pick the deck most likely to beat that field, even one you dislike: "Give it a try, it might turn out to be better than you imagine" ([PV's Playhouse: Tournament Preparation](https://articles.starcitygames.com/articles/pvs-playhouse-tournament-preparation/)). Reid Duke: "Understanding the metagame can be just as important as the general strategy of the game itself," and "what's happened in the past is the best indicator of what will happen in the future" ([Level One: The Metagame](https://magic.wizards.com/en/articles/archive/level-one/metagame-2015-06-01)). Karsten's Pro Tour tables are the proof: the decks that beat the Badgermole Cub field at Lorwyn Eclipsed were the ones with cheap instant-speed answers, and "the most-played decks generally underperformed, while newer builds did well" ([Karsten](https://magic.gg/news/metagame-mentor-standard-win-rates-and-lessons-from-pro-tour-lorwyn-eclipsed)).

**Tune, don't brew.** Andrew Elenbogen, after winning a Mythic Championship: "Never brew. Only tune stock decks," and lock the archetype a week out because "it dramatically reduces stress" ([How to Win a Mythic Championship](https://articles.starcitygames.com/articles/how-to-win-a-mythic-championship/)). Duke calls copying a proven list the norm because it buys time for tuning and practice, and says "to err on the side of sticking to one rather than switching back and forth" ([Choosing Your Deck](https://magic.wizards.com/en/articles/archive/level-one/choosing-your-deck-2015-06-08)). The exception that proves it: Team TCGplayer built Selesnya Ouroboroid the night before deck submission and its two pilots posted the highest win rate at Pro Tour Secrets of Strixhaven, 14–4 ([Karsten](https://magic.gg/news/metagame-mentor-standard-win-rates-and-lessons-from-pro-tour-secrets-of-strixhaven)). That is a full testing team's last-minute tune of a known archetype, not a brew.

**Play what you can pilot, at the speed the event demands.** Elenbogen: "If you can't win with a deck, it's irrelevant if that deck is broken in the hands of someone else." Damo da Rosa: "if you can't play a deck fast enough, don't play it." Zen Miyaji-Thorne's Pro Tour Aetherdrift choice was made by elimination on exactly those grounds: red aggro dismissed because he could not play it well enough, the big white decks because of paper time pressure, three-colour decks because of their mana, leaving Azorius Oculus even though he "did not consider it especially strong" ([Three for One Trading](https://www.threeforonetrading.com/en/pro-tour-aetherdrift-report)). At the top level Elenbogen also wants fewer decisions on both sides, since "Effects that are easy to play around generally get worse against top players."

**How a Pro Tour team prepares a Standard format.** Zen's Worldly Counsel team (about 17 people) started constructed testing two months out: one month of playing nearly every deck in leagues, weekly video calls to share results and matchups, Cockatrice for new cards as soon as spoilers appeared, then an in-house tournament split into two pods with every top deck and team deck in round-robin, followed by presentations of what each player learned ([Three for One Trading](https://www.threeforonetrading.com/en/pro-tour-aetherdrift-report)). Handshake rented houses outside Amsterdam for thirteen qualified members plus two helpers and spent the time trying to "develop an accurate conception of all our options"; Isaac Bullwinkle still switched decks four times on the Thursday before the GP ([Bullwinkle](https://isaacbullwinkle.substack.com/p/the-world-is-everything-there-is)). Javier Dominguez's team for Worlds 2024 aimed to "understand the Standard format well enough" that new cards would need only minor adjustments, and still chose wrong: "Sometimes you choose the wrong deck, but you can still take the trophy home" ([Hareruya](https://article.hareruyamtg.com/article/87728/?lang=en)). Three lessons recur in every report: the team's edge is breadth (someone has played every matchup from both sides), the metagame read is wrong more often than the deck is, and the final list is usually one or two cards different from the stock one.

**Where they disagree.** Best deck vs comfort: Damo da Rosa and Elenbogen say the field decides, Duke says "When you're having fun, and when you're passionate about a deck, you're going to perform better" and "You don't need to have the absolute best deck in order to do well in a tournament." Simple vs powerful: Elenbogen wants the deck with the fewest decisions at a Pro Tour, Seth Manfield says an unusual deck "out of left field" is "normally what I do the best with" ([magic.gg](https://magic.gg/news/the-week-that-was-a-lesson-from-the-world-champion)). Byes and risk: Damo da Rosa prefers "a deck that does so-so against everything" when he has byes and avoids decks with bad random-creature matchups when he doesn't.

## Play technique: the in-game skills the pros name

Constructed games are shorter and more scripted than Limited games, so the in-game edge is concentrated in a few repeatable decisions: the keep, the first three land drops, when to switch roles, and what to play around. In rough order of how often the experts raise them:

1.  **Mulligans, by matchup.** Reid Duke's constructed rules: "Mulligan decisions in Constructed are all about context, the way your deck matches up against your opponent's"; "Put a premium on having your best early plays in your opening hand"; "Mulligan more aggressively if either your deck, or your opponent's deck, is very fast"; and sideboard cards in hand are "a great tiebreaker in favor of mulliganing" a borderline seven ([Level One: Mulligans Part III](https://magic.wizards.com/en/articles/archive/level-one/mulligans-part-iii-constructed-2015-06-29)). The single largest measured in-game lever in Limited (about 14 points a mulligan) and almost certainly the same in constructed, where a six-card hand against a curve-out deck is a game mostly lost before it starts.

2.  **Knowing the matchup's critical turns.** Damo da Rosa: "you should always know what has to happen for you to lose or win a game" ([Tournament Preparation](https://articles.starcitygames.com/articles/pvs-playhouse-tournament-preparation/)). Jadine Klomparens's matchup method: name the key cards, then the secondary cards that let them dominate, because "Having a predetermined list of which cards are the best for you in a matchup can help to guide your play" ([A Practical Guide to Matchup Analysis](https://articles.starcitygames.com/articles/a-practical-guide-to-matchup-analysis/)).

3.  **Role assignment and turning the corner.** Mike Flores's "Misassignment of role equals game loss" is still the most-cited line in constructed strategy ([Who's the Beatdown?](https://articles.starcitygames.com/articles/whos-the-beatdown/)). Klomparens adds the timing rule for midrange mirrors, where the role is unclear: "the best time to turn the corner is as soon as you possibly can," since every extra turn is another draw for the opponent, and the only way to learn where that point is: "There's only one way: play games."

4.  **Holding cards for their best moment.** Jeremy Neeman on tight play: strong players "don't play their cards at the first opportunity," "Every decision they make is with the end in mind," and "the further ahead you are, the more you can afford to avoid risks"; behind, take chances and "make excellent trades whenever possible" ([Tight Plays](https://articles.starcitygames.com/articles/tight-plays/)). Life "is not a critical resource" until it is.

5.  **Land and spell sequencing.** Emma Handy: "Making sure they're played in the proper order matters a surprising amount," with the tapped land on the turn you have nothing to cast and the untapped one on the turn you do ([Land Sequencing](https://articles.starcitygames.com/articles/tips-and-tricks-for-proper-land-sequencing/)). Jacob Nagro on information: play lands after combat to give "your opponent less information for their decisions," and hold a land only against cards "you know your opponent has in their deck," because "sometimes your own deck can punish you for not playing your lands" ([Hareruya](https://article.hareruyamtg.com/article/22172/?lang=en)).

6.  **Playing around cards, selectively.** The pro consensus is to play around what you have seen or what the list is known to run, and otherwise to make the play that wins against the most likely hand. Elenbogen's corollary for deck building: at a Pro Tour, cards that are easy to play around get worse, because the opponents will.

7.  **Focus and recovery.** Dominguez's drill is to play every practice game as if it were a Grand Prix Top 8, and his rule after an error is to refocus at once rather than carry it: a missed land drop or a misplay "is fine, as long as you refocus right away" ([MTG Pro Tutor 47](https://mtgprotutor.libsyn.com/47-javier-dominguez-suggests-you-play-your-deck-a-ton-to-know-it-inside-and-out)). From his Worlds 2024 win: "it is crucial to stay focused, no matter how well or poorly things are going" ([Hareruya](https://article.hareruyamtg.com/article/87728/?lang=en)).

8.  **Fewer mistakes, not more brilliance.** MTG Arena Zone's framing, which most coaches echo: "Magic isn't a game of playing well so much as a game of trying to avoid playing poorly" ([Five Helpful Habits](https://mtgazone.com/five-helpful-habits-to-improve-at-magic-you-may-not-be-doing/)). Reid Duke's biggest-mistake answer: "Players try to do too much" ([MTG Pro Tutor 169](https://mtgprotutor.libsyn.com/podcast/169-reid-duke-on-how-to-prepare-for-success)).

The honest limit, as with Limited: nobody has published a same-deck, different-pilot study for constructed. The size of the play edge rests on the 10-point team-over-field gap, Duke's "pilot over deck" claim, Faust's 7–0 with a 46% archetype, and the Pro Tour pattern that prepared pilots of a stock deck beat the field's pilots of the same deck by 10–20 points. That is consistent with play and preparation together being worth as much as deck choice, and with neither being worth much without the other.

## Sideboarding and best-of-three: the hidden edge

In a best-of-three, at least half and often two thirds of the games are sideboarded, so the 15 cards and the plan for them matter as much as the 60. The pros treat this as the most learnable edge in the game, and the one most players leave on the table.

**Why it matters.** Brad Nelson: "Sideboarding is the most integral part of Magic for tournament success at the highest levels," and it matters more than main-deck perfection because it "can be mastered through work" ([A Guide to Sideboarding Correctly](https://articles.starcitygames.com/articles/a-guide-to-sideboarding-correctly/)). Nick Miller's 2024 round-up calls it "some of the most important and often underemphasized skills in the game" ([Five Must-Know Sideboarding Tips](https://articles.starcitygames.com/magic-the-gathering/five-must-know-sideboarding-tips-for-competitive-magic-the-gathering/)). Reid Duke: "Always go in with a plan," because a sideboard plan "is a way to unify your deck construction with your in-match decisions" ([Level One: Sideboard Plans](https://magic.wizards.com/en/articles/archive/level-one/sideboard-plans-2015-03-09)).

**The rules the experts agree on.**

| Rule                                                   | In their words                                                                                                                                      | Who                                                                                                                                                |
|--------------------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Board for their post-board deck, not their game-1 deck | "Sideboard for how their deck will be after they sideboard, and not just for their maindeck"; "Don't sideboard against the deck they had in Game 1" | Nelson, Duke ([SCG](https://articles.starcitygames.com/magic-the-gathering/five-must-know-sideboarding-tips-for-competitive-magic-the-gathering/)) |
| Don't over-board                                       | "People change so many cards that they compromise their main strategy"                                                                              | Duke (same)                                                                                                                                        |
| Think in 75, not 60 + 15                               | "You don't have a deck and a sideboard, you have a 75-card pool of cards to build new decks out of throughout the event"                            | Brian Coval (same)                                                                                                                                 |
| Build the sideboard for matchups, not cards            | "you should do so with matchups in mind, not particular cards in mind"                                                                              | [Duke](https://magic.wizards.com/en/articles/archive/level-one/sideboard-plans-2015-03-09)                                                         |
| Count the dispensable cards per matchup first          | "go through your main deck and count the number of cards that are dispensable"                                                                      | [Duke](https://magic.wizards.com/en/articles/archive/level-one/sideboard-plans-2015-03-09)                                                         |
| Full swaps, not trims                                  | "if you don't want one copy, you probably don't want any"; shaving is "the exact opposite of correct"                                               | [Nelson](https://articles.starcitygames.com/articles/a-guide-to-sideboarding-correctly/)                                                           |
| Different plans on the play and on the draw            | On the draw, cheap interaction; on the play, more proactive cards                                                                                   | Miller; Nelson                                                                                                                                     |
| Sometimes sacrifice a matchup                          | Fixing one bad matchup can cost flexibility against several others                                                                                  | [Miller](https://articles.starcitygames.com/magic-the-gathering/five-must-know-sideboarding-tips-for-competitive-magic-the-gathering/)             |
| Know how every deck in the format boards               | "You have to know every deck in the format inside and out"                                                                                          | [Nelson](https://articles.starcitygames.com/articles/a-guide-to-sideboarding-correctly/)                                                           |

**Role change after boarding.** Nelson's own pattern is to play "a zone defense" in game 1 and shift to aggressive interaction after boarding, and his Pro Tour story is pure metagaming of the sideboard: "They all prepared to go lower, so I went bigger." Klomparens sorts post-board cards into punishers (cards that punish the opponent's intended role) and enablers (cards that let your deck play its intended role), then re-reads the matchup with them in ([Matchup Analysis](https://articles.starcitygames.com/articles/a-practical-guide-to-matchup-analysis/)). Bullwinkle won a key Pro Tour match by keeping Supreme Verdict over Galvanic Discharge on the reasoning that, when the plan is unclear, keeping blue cards for pitch spells is usually right ([Bullwinkle](https://isaacbullwinkle.substack.com/p/the-world-is-everything-there-is)).

**How much is at stake.** Karsten's Pro Tour March of the Machine table has the clearest example of a deck losing to the field's sideboards: Esper Legends, 30 pilots, 42.3%, because "Lithomantic Barrage answered their threats too efficiently after sideboard" ([Karsten](https://magic.gg/news/metagame-mentor-the-highest-standard-win-rates-at-pro-tour-march-of-the)). At Lorwyn Eclipsed the most-played sideboard card, Soul-Guide Lantern, was aimed at Sultai Reanimator, which finished 36.7% over 31 pilots ([Karsten](https://magic.gg/news/metagame-mentor-standard-win-rates-and-lessons-from-pro-tour-lorwyn-eclipsed)). In both cases the losing deck's game-1 matchups were fine; the field's post-board plans decided the tournament for it.

**Play/draw in best-of-three.** The loser of each game chooses and nearly always plays. Miller and Nelson both build two plans per matchup for that reason, and Nelson treats being on the play as licence to take more risk in the build. The size of the on-the-play edge in current Standard is not public outside paid trackers; the usual working figure is 52–55%, larger for aggro mirrors and smaller for control.

## Practice, review and the mental game

The pros describe improvement as a loop of volume, honest review and discussion, with the review step being the one most players skip.

**Volume, against a real field.** Emma Handy: "Playing a copious number of games is the best way to prepare for a tournament. Period," with the worst matchups first and the goal to "explore as many angles of a matchup as possible," not to post the best practice win rate ([A Single Day of Playtesting](https://articles.starcitygames.com/articles/a-single-day-of-playtesting/)). Elenbogen does most of his testing on Magic Online and Arena against "a broad range of both decks and builds," and saves teammates for specific questions because they "will typically be noticeably better than the opposition you will find on the internet" ([SCG](https://articles.starcitygames.com/articles/how-to-win-a-mythic-championship/)). Dominguez's level-up moment was daily play with friends running "all decks against all decks," and his Standard advice is to pick a performing deck and play it enough to know it thoroughly ([MTG Pro Tutor 47](https://mtgprotutor.libsyn.com/47-javier-dominguez-suggests-you-play-your-deck-a-ton-to-know-it-inside-and-out)). Rob Dougherty's 2002 playtesting method still stands: test the deck as-is against the gauntlet before changing a card, then "Do best two out of three, with sideboarding after game one, just like in a tournament" ([Playtesting 101](https://articles.starcitygames.com/articles/playtesting-101-the-six-steps-of-evaluating-decks/)).

**Quality over quantity, and the list of mistakes.** Reid Duke's daily habit is "Quality practice over quantity," he starts serious preparation about three weeks out, and his lesson from a bad year was "to be honest with himself" and not cut corners ([MTG Pro Tutor 169](https://mtgprotutor.libsyn.com/podcast/169-reid-duke-on-how-to-prepare-for-success)). Sam Black on the turn in Duke's career: after a disappointing 2012 Players Championship, "Reid made a list of every mistake he made in the tournament," and "it all starts with the list"; Black's confession about why most players don't is that "I usually want to play another game right away" ([Do As I Say, Not As I Do](https://articles.starcitygames.com/articles/do-as-i-say-not-as-i-do-on-learning-the-way-i-imagine-reid-duke-does/)). From Duke's Limited-era advice, which applies unchanged: "More important than the number of hours you play is how much you're actually taking away from those hours," and after each game "ask yourself why the winning player won" ([Becoming a Better Player](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/feature/becoming-better-player-2015-09-28)).

**Record, review, and play the other side.** MTG Arena Zone's five habits: record games and review them later with fresh eyes ("Recording VODs gives you the security of knowing that you can't forget any context"); play the opposing deck, because "it's extremely difficult to understand how to approach a matchup from either side if you only play one half of it"; take "a small break after every 2 matches"; watch streams by predicting the streamer's play and asking why they deviated; and be honest about errors ([Five Helpful Habits](https://mtgazone.com/five-helpful-habits-to-improve-at-magic-you-may-not-be-doing/)). Testing guides add the recording discipline: for every game note the opponent's archetype, play or draw, mulligans, flood or screw, regretted decisions and whether the sideboard fit, and read the notes as a whole at the end rather than game by game ([Modern Nexus](https://modernnexus.com/systematic-and-practical-testing-guidelines/)).

**Talk about the game.** Dominguez's self-named biggest mistake: "not talking about the game with other people enough"; his fix was writing down draft picks and asking others whether they agreed, and finding a regular group to argue meta decisions with. Every Pro Tour report above ends the same way: a team's value is that someone else has played the matchup you haven't, and will tell you what you got wrong. Nathan Steuer, after winning Pro Tour Secrets of Strixhaven, credited Dominguez and teammates for the final list ([Karsten](https://magic.gg/news/metagame-mentor-standard-win-rates-and-lessons-from-pro-tour-secrets-of-strixhaven)).

**Tilt, variance and fatigue.** Braun-Duin's turn came from refusing the luck story: "You don't win more at dice by working at it," and "Being able to identify mistakes, make corrections, and then improve results is the sign of a skill format" ([The Skill in Standard](https://articles.starcitygames.com/articles/the-skill-in-standard/)). Duke's balance: "blaming bad luck all the time is unhealthy, but the opposite is dangerous as well." Ben Friedman's tournament rule is to set a goal of winning the next round, not making Top 8, and never to commiserate about luck between rounds ([Your Mind at a Tournament](https://articles.starcitygames.com/articles/your-mind-at-a-magic-tournament/)). Dominguez's physical rule: "Eat and drink at least every 2 hours. If you are hungry or thirsty then you've waited too long." Simon Nielsen, the 2023 Player of the Year, took a month off after burning out: "It was really healthy for me" ([magic.gg](https://magic.gg/news/simon-nielsen-2023-player-of-the-year)). The arithmetic is on their side: a 55% player needs 400–1,000 matches to prove it, so any one tournament's record says almost nothing about whether the preparation worked.

**What a career looks like.** Damo da Rosa on his own rise: "It's a very gradual thing, I kept getting better and at some point I was better than most other players" ([Hall of Fame](https://www.magic.gg/news/paulo-vitor-damo-da-rosa-hall-of-fame)). Budde: "For a long time I devoted most of my time to Magic" ([Hall of Fame](https://www.magic.gg/news/kai-budde-hall-of-fame)). Neither names a trick; both name time.

## What this means for the sim and the coach

The sim is a Standard tool, so these findings land directly on it. Read against Code's 12 Oct status (k = 0.07 on 1,063 real games; Forge underrates instant-based decks; a reactive pilot lifts Azorius Control from 13% to 27%; Monte Carlo search at 59% vs stock but 5 minutes a game), five things follow.

- **The sim's natural job is the part of the edge that is decided before round one.** Deck choice and build are worth 10–30 points at a Pro Tour and are a matter of reading the field, which is exactly what a matchup matrix over the top-16 meta with real-data calibration does. That is the product: "which of these lists is best positioned against this week's field," not "what is my win rate." The coach should say so, and should lead with the field (Karsten's shares and the RC results) rather than with the brew.

- **A strong pilot on both sides should raise k, and the ceiling is the field's skill, not the pros'.** The real data comes from RCQ and Challenge fields, where pilots are 5–10 points weaker than the Pro Tour teams; Karsten's tables show that a stock deck in prepared hands beats the same deck in the field's hands by 10–20 points. So Code's question 2 has a likely answer: a pilot that is merely competent on both sides (holds up counters, keeps the right seven, boards correctly) should move Forge toward the RCQ field, and a pilot at team level would overshoot it. The test is the one Code is already running: does Azorius Control's real-vs-Forge gap close as the pilot improves, and does Lifegain's overrating shrink at the same time.

- **Sideboarding is where the sim can be most unlike Forge and most like a pro.** Two of three games are boarded; sideboard.js already matches real pilots on 91% of planned copies; the bridge now runs real between-game sideboarding. The upgrades the experts name are cheap: two plans per matchup (on the play, on the draw), full swaps rather than trims, and a check that the boarded 60 still has its mana and curve (Duke's dispensable-card count). The audit endpoint should report disagreement with real sideboards per matchup, not just agreement.

- **Mulligans by matchup, not by land count.** Duke's four constructed rules (early plays first, harder against fast decks, looser against slow ones, sideboard cards as tiebreakers) are a rule set the pilot can encode in an afternoon, and they are matchup-aware where Forge's are not. Code found the colour-aware rule gave no self-play gain; the matchup-aware version is the one the pros actually use, and it should be measured on the aggro pairings where it matters most.

- **Role assignment is the next decision for search.** Klomparens's method is the spec: name the key cards per matchup, decide who is the beatdown from the two lists, and turn the corner as early as the board allows. A flat Monte Carlo that only searches combat on turns where the evaluator's spread is wide is the affordable version of "play games to find the corner," and it is the answer to Code's question 1 about where to spend play-outs.

For the people using it: the coach's job after a run is Duke's question, why did the winner win in this matchup with these cards, and its job before a run is Damo da Rosa's: what has to happen for this deck to win or lose this matchup. The numbers it shows should carry the same honesty the pros carry about their own results, a range on every rate, the real-game count behind every calibration, and no single run read as a verdict.
