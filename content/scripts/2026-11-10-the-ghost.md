## Spoken

Four skies looked at one fuzzy smudge and saw a nostril, a ghost, a cow's udder, and a manger.

This is Praesepe, in Cancer. To the eye it is a patch of haze. Through any lens at all it is a thousand stars, six hundred light years out.

The Arabs called it al-Nathra, the nostril, the tip of their lion's nose.

The Chinese called the station Guǐ, ghost, and the haze inside it the vapour of piled corpses.

India called it Puṣya, the nourisher, and drew it as a cow's udder, and as a lotus.

The Greeks called it the Manger, with two donkeys feeding at it. Two stars beside it are still named the asses.

Everyone named the smudge. Nobody named the stars inside it. Nobody could, until Galileo turned a telescope on it and counted more than forty.

China saw vapour. India saw milk. The same soft white patch, feeding something.

Of the Moon's 28 stations, this is one of fourteen where every sky agrees.

## Facts checked

- al-Nathra, "the nostril" of the great Arab lion; 鬼 Guǐ, the 23rd xiu, with the cluster recorded as 積尸氣, the vapour of piled corpses; Puṣya, "the nourisher", symbol the cow's udder and the lotus, deity Bṛhaspati (`research/mansions-table.json`, id 8; `research/corpus-mansions.md` `MANSION.08`).
- Praesepe, Latin "manger"; γ and δ Cancri are Asellus Borealis and Asellus Australis, the northern and southern donkey. The cluster lies about 580 light years away and holds about a thousand stars.
- Galileo's Sidereus Nuncius (1610) reports Praesepe resolved into more than forty stars.
- "One of fourteen": fourteen stations carry the STRONG flag (`CONTENT.md`, the agreement map).
