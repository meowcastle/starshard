# Manzil, explained to a friend

*6 October 2026. The plain version of `MANZIL-TCG.md`. No research, no citations — just
what the game is now, and what it becomes.*

---

## The one-line pitch

**It's a card game where your opponent is tonight's actual sky.**

The 28 cards are the 28 "mansions" of the Moon — the houses the Moon passes through each
month, which Arabic, Indian, Chinese and Japanese astronomers all mapped out independently
over a thousand years ago. The opponent plays the planets, and the planets are placed where
they really are in the sky right now. Your deck comes from your own birth chart. So the game
is different every night because the world is, and no two people start with the same cards.

That's the idea. Here's how it actually plays — today, and after the overhaul.

---

## Part one: Manzil as it exists today

### How a game goes

You see a row of nine spaces — "the road." Each space is one of the 28 mansions, nine in a
row, starting from wherever the Moon is tonight.

You hold five cards. Every card has two numbers, one on the left and one on the right, and a
small special ability. You and the sky take turns placing one card into an empty space.

When you place a card, it fights its neighbours. Your card's left number faces the card to
its left; your right number faces the card to its right. **Bigger number wins and flips the
neighbour to your side.** If the numbers tie, it flips anyway — and a card flipped by a tie
immediately attacks *its* neighbours too, so one placement can start a chain reaction down
the road.

A card placed on its own mansion counts double at the end. The sky's planets have abilities
too: Saturn can't be flipped, Mars chains onward, Venus weakens whatever lands beside her.

When all nine spaces are full, whoever owns more wins the board. Best of five boards wins
the night.

### What you're doing between nights

You start with the five mansions from your birth chart. Each unclaimed mansion has a night
when the Moon is in it, and on that night you climb a ladder — eight other players' hands,
each one a bit sharper than the last, then the sky herself at the top. Win, and the mansion
is yours. Collecting all 28 is the long game.

Cards level up as you play them. As they level, the text on the card fades away — first the
name goes, then the ability text — until a maxed card is mostly just art. The idea is that by
then you *know* it. Your deck ends up looking like no one else's.

### Why it's good

It's fast — a board is two minutes. The core rule fits in one sentence. The theme is real:
those really are the planets, that really is tonight. And the chain reactions are satisfying
when they go off.

### Why it isn't good enough yet

We ran the game through a simulator about ninety thousand times and watched a lot of MTG
Arena. Four problems, honestly stated:

1. **It's solved.** With your starting five cards, a handful of specific opening moves win
   every single board, on every night of the month. Once you know them, there's no game.
2. **It gets smaller as you play.** Nine spaces, five cards. Every card you place removes a
   space. By your last card you often have one legal move. The game *narrows* toward its
   ending, which is exactly backwards for tension — Magic does the opposite, giving you more
   options every turn.
3. **Every card costs the same.** There are no cheap cards or expensive cards, so there's no
   build-up, no "I'm saving this one," no reason the big card feels big.
4. **Cards act once and then they're just a number.** You place it, it fights, done. Nothing
   you own grows, nothing improves anything else. In Arena we watched a 2/2 creature become a
   12/10 over a game because other cards kept feeding it. Nothing like that can happen here.

Plus a smaller one: the sky's moves happen silently. When a chain reaction goes off you don't
really know why. Arena narrates everything — a label on every move, a glowing line from cause
to effect. Our best mechanic is our least readable moment.

## Part two: the overhaul

Same sky, same 28 mansions, same two numbers on every card, same flip rule. Everything below
is built *on* that, not instead of it.

### The road moves

This is the big one.

Instead of nine spaces that fill up and end, **the road scrolls like the night itself.** Nine
mansions are visible at once. At the end of each round, the leftmost mansion **sets** — like
a star going below the horizon. Whoever holds it at that moment banks it. Then the next
mansion in the real sequence **rises** on the right, and if a planet is actually standing in
that mansion tonight, it arrives already there.

So the road never fills. You're always choosing among nine places, start to finish. A card
you place doesn't score when you place it — it scores when its mansion sets, several rounds
later, *if you still hold it*. Which means holding ground matters, stealing a card back the
round before it sets is a real move, and a strong night can bank far more than nine.

**What it feels like:** less like filling in a grid, more like defending a position while
the ground moves under you. And the sky isn't a skin anymore — what rises next and what's
standing in it is literally tonight's astronomy.

### You can stack

A card can be placed on top of an occupied mansion if it's "higher" (every card already has
an elevation; this just makes it matter). The card underneath is **occulted** — covered, still
yours, dormant. If the top card gets flipped, the one underneath is uncovered and fights
again as if freshly placed.

So no space ever truly closes, cards can act more than once, and one flip can swing two or
three cards at once.

### Your opponent gets to answer

Two small additions, both named for real sky events:

- **The veil.** You can keep one card face-down on the road. It reveals and fights when
  someone places next to it, or when its mansion sets. A hidden answer.
- **The retrograde.** Twice a night, when your opponent places a card, you can flip which of
  its numbers faces which way — *before* the fight resolves.

This is what Arena's combat has that Manzil didn't: when you commit, the other side gets a
say, and neither of you fully controls the outcome.

### Cards have a cost, and the sky sets the prices

Each round you get a little more to spend — round one you have one, round five you have
five. Cards cost between zero and five. So the first play is small and the last play is big.
That's the arc that was missing.

And here's the Manzil twist: **cards belong to a planet — Saturn, Mars, Venus, Mercury or
Jupiter — and a planet's cards are cheaper on nights when that planet is strong in the real
sky.** Some nights Mars is on sale and Venus is dear. The sky doesn't just place pieces; it
changes what's affordable. Every night is a slightly different game before anyone draws a
card.

### More kinds of card

Today there's one kind. The overhaul has five:

- **Mansions** — the 28 places. The permanent cast. What you have now.
- **Figures** — the characters. Every mansion comes with ones the old traditions already gave
  it: the Heart has Antares the red star, an amulet from the Indian sky, a fox from the
  Chinese one. Figures have numbers and *do things* — when placed, when flipped, when a
  planet moves next to them.
- **Talismans** — attach to a card you've placed and make it better. If the card is flipped,
  the talisman survives and stays yours. This is how things compound.
- **Aspects** — instant responses. Played when something happens, including on the other
  side's turn.
- **Omens** — the twelve zodiac signs. Set one and it changes the rules for the whole road
  while it stands.

### Your deck comes from your chart, and it's led by you

Every deck has a leader card. **Yours is read from your own birth chart** — the mansion the
Moon was in when you were born, or the planet that rules it. Your leader decides which
planet's cards your deck can run freely (others cost extra). It's always available, and it
comes back when it's flipped.

This is the thing that makes it personal all the way down. Your friend's deck and yours
aren't legal for each other, because you weren't born under the same sky.

### Ways to play

Same cards, different rules, so it never goes stale:

- **Tonight's Sky** — the daily. Three minutes against the real sky, with one special rule
  that changes depending on which mansion the Moon is in tonight. This is the front door.
- **Ascendant** — the main event. Your chart-leader, your singleton deck, head to head. This
  is the one people will build an identity around — think Commander in Magic.
- **Sealed Night** — you get nine random mansions plus your Moon, build five, play. For when
  you don't want to think about deckbuilding.
- **Draft the Sky** — two to four people take turns picking from the twelve mansions visible
  tonight. The leftovers become the opponent.
- **The Year** — 28 nights in a row with one leader, each night authored, building toward
  something. The solo campaign.

### The cards are printed once — honestly

Every card you need to play is always available at a fixed price, forever. No packs, no
gambling, no "last chance."

What's printed once is the **Night Edition**: the card for a specific night — *the Heart, 14
March 2027* — carrying that night's real sky on it, dated, numbered. You get it by playing
that night, or by ordering it during a short window after. Then it's closed, and the count is
published. It plays identically to the regular card. It isn't rarer in the game; it's proof
you were there under that sky.

That's the whole collectible layer, and it's built so nobody can get burned by it.

### What narrates

Every move the sky makes gets a plain-language line. Every flip in a chain reaction draws a
line from the card that caused it to the card it hit. Every card with a condition shows
whether the condition is live right now. The board wears tonight's mansion. The opponents
have names and faces. None of this changes the rules; all of it changes whether you understand
what just happened — which turned out to be most of what Arena does better.

## The experience, in one night

You open the app. Tonight the Moon is in the Empty District, so the road starts there, and
tonight's rule is in effect. Mars is strong this week, so your Mars cards are cheap; you build
around that. Your leader is the Crown, because that's where your Moon was when you were born.

Round one, you've got one to spend. You drop a small Figure on a mansion that won't set for a
while. The sky places Saturn two spaces over. Round two, you lay a Talisman on your Figure.
The sky places Venus beside it and softens it. You veil a card next to Venus and say nothing.

Round three the leftmost mansion sets — the sky held it, she banks one. A new mansion rises on
the right with Jupiter already standing in it. You spend a retrograde to flip Jupiter's
numbers before he fights, and your veiled card reveals and takes the mansion beside him. The
chain runs two more cards. Three to one, your way.

Six rounds, the Moon has crossed six mansions, the night ends. You banked more. The Crown
levels, its name fades off the card. And because it's the 14th, the version of the Empty
District you played with tonight now exists, dated, with tonight's sky on it, numbered,
yours.

Tomorrow the Moon is somewhere else, the rule is different, Mars is a little weaker, and the
road starts one mansion further on.

## What's the same, what's new — one table

| | today | after |
|---|---|---|
| the board | nine spaces that fill up | nine spaces that scroll with the Moon |
| when you score | when the board is full | when a mansion sets, if you hold it |
| your cards | five fixed, all cost the same | a deck with a curve; cost rises each round |
| who sets the prices | nobody | the real sky, nightly |
| kinds of card | one | five |
| does anything compound | no | talismans, counters, stacking |
| can the opponent answer | no | veil and retrograde |
| your deck's identity | your five chart cards | a leader read from your chart |
| ways to play | the nightly climb | five formats on the same cards |
| collecting | 28 mansions | 28 mansions, plus the night you played them |
| the sky | decoration | the engine |

None of the new version exists yet. The old simulator is the base, the moving road is the
first thing to build, and the same honesty applies as always: test it against the bands
before believing any of it.
