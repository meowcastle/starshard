### 1.2 The popularity data, sorted by source quality

- **Hard numbers from Wizards**: 70% of fans play Commander (investor webinar, Dec 2022); 28,000 unique weekly in-store players (Feb 2020); 61 Commander events/yr at WPN Premium stores. ([Wargamer](https://www.wargamer.com/magic-the-gathering/casual-players-wizards-strategy), [SCG](https://articles.starcitygames.com/news/new-data-reveals-massive-growth-in-commander-playerbase/))
- **Qualified designer statements**: Rosewater, 2023: "the number one played format in tabletop" and he credits Wizards' product support for that ([MTG Rocks](https://mtgrocks.com/mtg-lead-designer-reveals-why-commander-is-so-popular/)); Rosewater, Aug 2026: not a majority of all games played ([ICv2](https://icv2.com/print/article/63166)).
- **Common but unsourced**: "Commander is more than half of Magic sales" / "half of all Magic played." I found no Hasbro or Wizards statement giving a sales share. **[unverified]** The strongest true version is "70% of fans play it" (participation, not share of games or revenue).
- **Tournament vs casual**: Commander has essentially no sanctioned tournament circuit; Forsythe (Oct 2024) noted banlist decisions are "all sentiment-driven for the most part" because there is no tournament data. cEDH exists as a community-run competitive layer (Bracket 5).

### 1.3 Why it won: the design levers

Rosewater's own post-mortem, written the week the first precons shipped ([Word Commander, 20 Jun 2011](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/word-commander-2011-06-20)):

1. **The commander as identity**: "probably the most brilliant thing about it... people don't connect with ideas, they connect with people." The legendary creature *personifies* the deck.
2. **Singleton**: "ramps up the randomness" and "makes players dig deeper when building decks."
3. **100 cards**: "adds a lot of randomness in a very subtle way," so games differ without the player feeling cheated by variance.
4. **Color identity**: "restrictions do a lot of good for a game" (he dislikes the fiddly execution but endorses the principle). The commander *is* the deckbuilding constraint.
5. **40 life, multiplayer**: more "time to develop" board states; games become a social event rather than a race.
6. **Ego investment**: "restrictions breed creativity"; the player bonds with the deck before the first game.

Add from the RC's Philosophy Document ([2017](https://articles.starcitygames.com/articles/exclusive-updated-commander-philosophy-document/)): the format is explicitly **casual-first**, the ban list "shapes philosophy rather than achieving balance," and **Rule 0** (local groups override everything) is a feature, not a bug. The 2024 collapse shows the flip side: a volunteer committee governing a product that moves hundreds of millions of dollars cannot survive a single unpopular decision once card prices are attached to it (Sable's three failures: communication, unclear mandate, post-Sheldon leadership).

Lesson for Manzil: the identity piece and the social contract piece are separable from Magic's scale. The governance piece is a warning: whoever controls legality controls prices and will absorb the anger.

## 2. Format design as a lever

Each Magic format is one rule change on the same card pool. Grouped by who made it and whether it caught on. Primary reference: [Wikipedia: MTG formats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magic:_The_Gathering_formats), [mtg.wiki](https://mtg.wiki).

### 2.1 Constructed: the single rule is "which cards are legal"

| Format | One rule | Origin | Date |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Standard** (Type 2) | Only the last ~2-3 years of sets; rotates yearly | Wizards | 1995 |
| **Vintage** (Type 1) | Everything legal; small *restricted* (1-of) list instead of bans | Wizards | 1994-95 |
| **Legacy** (Type 1.5) | Everything legal, banned list only | Wizards; given its own banlist separate from Vintage in Sept 2004 **[date from memory; Wikipedia summary says 2006]** | 2004 |
| **Modern** | 8th Edition (2003, modern card frame) forward; never rotates | Wizards (Tom LaPille), but preceded by Gavin Verhey's player-made "Overextended" on MTGO, which Wizards credited and which got Verhey hired. Announced Aug 2011; Pro Tour Philadelphia three weeks later. | 2011 |
| **Pioneer** | Return to Ravnica (2012) forward; never rotates. Stated reason: Modern "no longer serves the needed role of 'format where you can use your Standard cards after rotation.'" Five fetchlands pre-banned. | Wizards | 21 Oct 2019 |
| **Pauper** | Only cards ever printed at common | Players on Magic Online (Pauper Deck Challenge community); MTGO support 2008; paper-sanctioned and unified Jun 2019; Pauper Format Panel (community advisers) Jan 2022 | 2008 / 2019 |
| **Brawl** | 60-card singleton, Standard-legal, commander; 25 life 1v1 | Wizards (Gerritt Turner) | Mar 2018 |
| **Oathbreaker** | 60-card singleton, planeswalker commander plus a "signature spell" in the command zone, 20 life, 3-5 players | Players (Weirdcards Charitable Club, Minnesota, ~2017); Wizards recognized 15 Mar 2023 | 2017 / 2023 |
| **cEDH** | Commander rules, zero social contract; win-rate optimized | Players (community, mid-2010s) | ~2015 |
| **Duel Commander** | Commander rules, 1v1, 20 life, own banlist | Players (French judge Kevin Desprez) | ~2010s |
| **Tiny Leaders** | 50-card singleton, commander, every card MV <= 3, 25 life | Players; Wizards added to FNM late 2014 | 2014 |

### 2.2 Multiplayer and supplemental: the single rule is "a new object or a new seating"

| Format | One rule | Origin |
|---|---|---|
| **Two-Headed Giant** | Teams of two share one 30-life total, take turns together | Wizards, sanctioned 2005 |
| **Planechase** | A shared deck of oversized Plane cards changes the global rules; a planar die lets you "planeswalk" | Wizards, 2009 |
| **Archenemy** | One player at 40 life with a Scheme deck vs three allies | Wizards, 2010 |
| **Conspiracy** | A booster set designed to be *drafted* with cards that act during the draft itself; 3+ player free-for-all | Wizards, 2014 |
| **Commander** | see Section 1 | Players, 1990s; Wizards 2011 |

### 2.3 What catches on and what does not

**Successes that came from players (Commander, Pauper, Cube, Oathbreaker):**
- Each solved a *problem the publisher could not see or did not prioritise*: Commander (what do I do with 20 years of cards and four friends?), Pauper (what if I have no money?), Cube (what if I want to draft but there is no new set?), Overextended/Modern (what if I want Extended that never rotates?).
- Each has a **one-sentence rule** a child can repeat. Commander: "100 cards, one of each, led by a legend." Pauper: "commons only." Cube: "draft from a box I built."
- Each is **evergreen**: the constraint does not expire, so the deck you built keeps working. Cliff Daigle on Commander: "Aside from bannings, the cards don't change, and you can keep cards in a deck forever." ([MTGPrice, 2015](https://blog.mtgprice.com/?p=8686))
- Each grew a **governing community before the publisher arrived** (RC, Pauper Deck Challenge, cube forums). Wizards' pattern since 2019 is to recognise and create a panel rather than invent.

**Failures (Tiny Leaders, paper Brawl, Frontier):**
- **Tiny Leaders** (2014-15): the small card pool was *solved in weeks*; it became "a halfway-decent Legacy deck that has a Commander to cast on turn 3"; casual players left when the fun cards were the weak cards, and competitive players had Modern/Legacy already. A format that targets *both* audiences and serves neither. ([TappedOut, Jul 2016](https://tappedout.net/mtg-articles/2016/jul/8/what-happened-tiny-leaders))
- **Brawl in paper** (2018): launched with a degenerate commander (Baral) in a tiny pool; "games are not being played simply because there exists an option to say 'No' to everything"; mono-red then dominated; reputational damage stuck even after fixes; Wizards marketed it only to Commander players. Survived only digitally on Arena where the pool is curated. ([MTGGoldfish, 2018](https://beta.mtggoldfish.com/articles/why-brawl-is-dead-and-why-it-shouldn-t-be))
- Pattern: **a format dies when its pool is small enough to solve and its rules are not distinctive enough to make the solved state interesting.** Commander escaped this because 100-card singleton plus four players makes the "solved" deck still produce different games.

**Lesson for Manzil:** a 28-card pool is *tiny*; without a variance engine (daily sky, hidden information, draft) any constructed format will be solved in a weekend. The formats that must carry weight are the ones that inject externally-sourced variation (Section 5) or asymmetry (Section 6).

## 3. Limited (Draft and Sealed) as a permanent variety engine

### 3.1 Why "the purest form of the game"

Rosewater's "History of Drafting" podcast ([Drive to Work #1195](https://podscripts.co/podcasts/magic-the-gathering-drive-to-work-podcast/1195-the-history-of-drafting)): Rochester Draft came out of the playtest group (Strat-O-Matic fans) during development; R&D deliberately put draft on the Pro Tour in early 1996 (PT Los Angeles ran both Rochester and Booster Draft) and Booster Draft won because it is faster and private, which reduces intimidation. Rosewater: drafting "is the purest form of Magic" because the player is tested on card evaluation, reading signals, and deckbuilding *under uncertainty*, not on owning the right cards. Since *Mirage* (1996) every set has been designed for draft; Erik Lauer's *Innistrad* (2011) formalised the **signpost uncommon** that tells you what each two-color pair does.

### 3.2 How set design serves draft

- **Ten two-color archetypes**: every modern set gives each of the 10 color pairs a named deck (e.g. "WU fliers", "BR sacrifice"), signposted at uncommon and supported at common. Rosewater confirmed in May 2026 that upcoming sets return to ten after mid-size sets experimented with five focused pairs. ([Draftsim on Rosewater's blog, 13 May 2026](https://draftsim.com/mtg-maro-archetype-announcement-blog/))
- **As-fan**: R&D jargon for "how many cards of a theme show up when you fan a booster." "If your theme's not at common, it's not your theme." Typical targets: ~2 gold cards per booster in a multicolor set. Designers tune as-fan by *slot*, not by printing more rares. ([mtg.wiki](https://mtg.wiki/page/As-fan))
- **Commons curve**: commons carry the mana curve and the removal; rares are "surprise," not structure. The Play Booster (Feb 2024, *Murders at Karlov Manor*) cut commons per pack from 10 to 7 and so cut the common sheet from 101 to **81** and raised uncommons from 80 to **100**, explicitly to keep each individual common's frequency about the same while giving more answers to rares. ([mtg.wiki Play Booster](https://mtg.wiki/page/Play_Booster)) A Play Booster: 6 commons + 1 common/List slot + 3 uncommons + 1 rare/mythic + 2 wildcards + 1 land = 14.

### 3.3 The math (worked, not sourced)

Take a representative 2024-26 main set: ~81 commons, ~100 uncommons, ~60 rares, ~20 mythics = ~261 distinct cards.

- A **draft** gives you 3 x 14 = 42 cards, roughly 39 non-basic-land. A 40-card deck is ~23 spells + 17 lands. Ways to pick 23 spells from your 39: C(39,23) ≈ **3.8 x 10^10**. In practice colour discipline collapses this to a few hundred sensible builds per pool, but the *pool itself* is different every seat every draft.
- A **sealed** pool is 6 packs (~78 non-land cards): C(78,23) ≈ **3.5 x 10^19** raw 23-card subsets.
- Across the **whole set**, the number of 23-card spell suites is C(261,23) ≈ **5 x 10^32** (commons-and-uncommons only: ≈ 8 x 10^28). The point is not that these are all good; it is that a 261-card set, drafted, never repeats, which is why Wizards can sell the same box for three months.
- For comparison, Manzil's **28-card pool** yields C(28,5) = **98,280** five-card hands; 28 natal "commanders" with 8 singletons from the remaining 27 gives 28 x C(27,8) = **62 million** decks; multiplying by 28 authored night rules gives ~2.75 million distinct (hand, rule) states for the daily alone. Small pools are not hopeless; they just need the variation to come from *rules and asymmetry* instead of card count.

(Academic work on draft deck strength exists, e.g. [arXiv 2607.04782, "Predicting Drafted Deck Strength"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.04782), built on [17lands](https://www.17lands.com) data; I could not extract its diversity figures. **[unverified]**)

### 3.4 Cube: the player-made Limited format

Cube drafting originated in Toronto in the late 1990s (Brett Allen's snazzorama site is the oldest online resource); 2005-06 articles by Noah Weil, Sam Gomersall, Tom LaPille and Evan Erwin spread it; the 2007 Magic Invitational put it on camera; LaPille's "A Boy and His Cube" (Jun 2009) was the Wizards blessing; MTGO added Cube in 2012 and Vintage Cube became a recurring event; Arena got Cube in Apr 2020. A cube is 360-720 singleton cards you curate; the *one rule* is "draft from a fixed, curated pool instead of random boosters." It is the clearest proof that **a fixed pool plus drafting is itself a format**, and the most portable idea in this memo for a 28-card game. ([riptidelab timeline](https://riptidelab.com/forum/threads/a-brief-timeline-of-cube-history.185/latest), [mtg.wiki Cube](https://mtg.wiki/page/Cube))

## 4. Other games' format ecosystems

### 4.1 Hearthstone (Blizzard)
- **Tavern Brawl** (15 Jun 2015): "a whole new way to play that changes every week"; preset decks, custom construction rules, or global buffs. It is the purest example of a **rotating-rule format as a variety machine**: zero new cards, one new rule per week. ([patch notes](https://outof.games/realms/hearthstone/patches/36-tavern-brawl-launch))
- **Standard / Wild** (Apr 2016): Standard = last two years plus Core set; Wild = everything. Copied from Magic, and the model Lorcana and Pokémon follow.
- **Arena** (launch, 2014): pick-one-of-three draft; **Battlegrounds** (Nov 2019): an 8-player autobattler using the HS card art and almost none of its rules, now arguably the game's most-played mode **[unverified share]**; **Classic** (2021-23) and **Twist** (Jun 2023 - May 2026, curated rotating pools) were both retired. ([hearthstone.wiki.gg formats](https://hearthstone.wiki.gg/wiki/Game_format))
- Lesson: Blizzard's successful modes change the *rules* (Brawl, Battlegrounds); its failures changed only the *legal list* (Classic, Twist).

### 4.2 Pokémon TCG
- Annual Standard rotation keyed to a **regulation mark** printed on the card (letter), not to set names: 2026 rotation (announced 9 Jan 2026, live 10 Apr 2026) drops "G" and keeps H/I/J. Expanded (Black & White forward) is the eternal format. Legibility is the lesson: a child can look at the card and know if it is legal. ([PokeGuardian](https://www.pokeguardian.com/2925613_pokemon-tcg-standard-format-rotation-announced-for-april-10-2026-cards-with-g-regulation-mark-no-longer-legal))

### 4.3 Yu-Gi-Oh! (Konami): the cautionary tale
- One sanctioned format (**Advanced**) with no rotation since 1999-2002; all variety must come from new cards, which forces **power creep**: by 2024 games "compressed to 2-3 turns" and play is about stopping the opponent from playing at all (Ash Blossom as the exemplar). ([Nintendojo editorial, 5 Mar 2024](https://www.nintendojo.com/?p=205168))
- Players built retro "**Time Wizard**" formats (Goat 2005, Edison 2010) unofficially; in Jan 2022 Konami finally announced five side formats (Time Wizard, Heart of the Underdog, Common Charity, Deck Master, Rivalry of Warlords). Cardmarket's review: Yu-Gi-Oh! "lacking any sort of alternative for when the format gets stale was always a huge downside." ([Cardmarket, 3 Jan 2022](https://insight.cardmarket.com/en/Articles/Konami-Made-Five-New-Formats)) Konami's other answers were *separate games* (Speed Duel, Rush Duel) rather than formats on the same pool.
- Lesson: with one format, the only dial you have is card power, and that dial only turns one way.

### 4.4 Flesh and Blood (Legend Story Studios)
- **Classic Constructed** (60 cards + hero, 40 life) and **Blitz** (40 cards, young hero, 20 life) are the two constructed formats on one pool.
- **Living Legend**: a hero accumulates points from top finishes; at **1,000 points the hero and their signature weapon retire** from Classic Constructed into the Living Legend format, while the class's other cards stay legal. James White: the goal is "a home for any Flesh and Blood card that we ever print." It is rotation by *merit* rather than by *date*, and it rotates the *identity* (hero) rather than the pool. ([SCG interview with James White](https://articles.starcitygames.com/flesh-and-blood/flesh-and-blood-creator-talks-living-legend-format-rosetta-design-uzuri-winning-the-pro-tour/), [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flesh_and_Blood_(card_game)))
- Directly relevant to Manzil: a commander-like structure can rotate the *commander* and leave the 99.

### 4.5 Disney Lorcana (Ravensburger)
- First rotation 5 Sep 2025: sets 1-4 left **Core Constructed** with the release of set 9 (*Fabled*, which reprinted key cards); four sets rotate each Q3 thereafter; **Infinity Constructed** keeps everything legal. Framed as what "mainstream TCGs that have reached a mature age" do. ([lorcana.gg](https://lorcana.gg/rotation/), [Disney Lorcana release notes](https://www.disneylorcana.com/en-US/news/2025/08/2025-8-release-notes-fabled))

### 4.6 Marvel Snap (Second Dinner)
- The whole game is a **location-rule engine**: 12-card decks, three locations each with its own rule revealed over turns 1-3; Brode: "random locations force on-the-fly adaptation." **Featured Location** every Tuesday (new location, 40% appearance for 48h) and **Hot Location** every Saturday (existing location, 60% for 24h). **Conquest** (Jun 2023) adds a best-of-N ladder (Proving Grounds, Silver, Gold, Infinite) as the "ultra competitive" counterweight; failed mode experiments become limited-time events. ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marvel_Snap), [GameSpot](https://www.gamespot.com/articles/marvel-snap-featured-and-hot-locations-explained/1100-6511164/), [ONE Esports, Oct 2023](https://www.oneesports.gg/gaming/ben-brode-marvel-snap-conquest-new-modes/))
- Snap is the closest *commercial* analogue to "the sky's temper": the board itself carries the weekly rule.

### 4.7 Legends of Runeterra (Riot)
- Mid-2022: Riot deprioritised the PvE **Path of Champions** to "refocus on PvP." Jan 2024: reversed. "LoR has faced financial challenges since launch, costing significantly more to develop and support than it generates"; team shrunk and "renewing our focus on The Path of Champions," because that is where players spent the most time. ([outof.games, 2022](https://outof.games/news/4872-legends-of-runeterra-is-refocusing-on-pvp-path-of-champions-put-on-backburner-riot-developers-joining-other-teams/), [Riot 2024 player update](https://www.riotgames.com/en/news/2024-player-update))
- Lesson: a generous, well-balanced PvP card game can still fail to monetise; the roguelike single-player *format* (one champion, build a deck across a run, random relics) was the thing players actually wanted. For Manzil, whose default opponent is already "the sky tonight," the PvE format is not a fallback; it is the front door.

## 5. Daily / rotating rule as format: FFXIV Triple Triad

FFXIV's Triple Triad (Gold Saucer, 2015) is a 3x3, five-card game with ~451 cards as of patch 7.35, and it stays fresh almost entirely through **rules layered per opponent**. Source for all rule text: [FFXIV Console Games Wiki](https://ffxiv.consolegameswiki.com/wiki/Triple_Triad).

**Two rule layers:**
- **Match rules** are fixed per NPC (each NPC "is" a ruleset: this card-collector plays *Same + Plus*, that pirate plays *Chaos + Reverse*).
- **Regional rules** apply to a whole zone and **rotate daily at 7am PST**, so the same NPC plays differently tomorrow.

**The variants and what each one does to the same board:**

| Rule | Text | What it changes |
|---|---|---|
| Same | Two or more adjacent sides equal: capture all | Rewards building symmetric walls; makes low cards useful |
| Plus | Two or more adjacent sides sum to the same total: capture all | Arithmetic puzzle; ranks matter less than pairs |
| Combo | Captures from Same/Plus chain into normal captures | Turns one placement into a cascade; swingy endgames |
| Ascension | Each faction card placed gives +1 to all cards of that faction (both decks) | Tribal synergy; deck composition matters |
| Descension | Faction cards placed give -1 to that faction | Punishes tribal decks; mixed decks win |
| Order | Must play cards in deck order | Removes hand choice; shifts skill to deck sequencing |
| Chaos | Each turn's card is chosen at random | Removes hand choice entirely; skill moves to placement |
| Swap | One card exchanged with the opponent before play | Imperfect ownership; your best card may be theirs |
| Roulette | The advanced rules are chosen at random | Meta-rule; you build for robustness |
| Three Open | Three cards of each hand revealed | Partial information; bluff space |
| All Open | All five cards revealed | Perfect information; pure placement puzzle |
| Fallen Ace | A '1' captures an 'A' | Inverts the top of the curve only |
| Reverse | Lower numbers win | Full inversion; "bad" cards become good |
| Sudden Death | Draws replay with the captured cards | Changes risk tolerance in close games |
| Draft | Pick from three random sets instead of your deck | Limited format for tournaments (Open/Invitational use it) |

**Deckbuilding rules** are themselves a constructed format: five cards, max two 4-star, max one 5-star; NPCs ignore the limits (asymmetric opponent, like Archenemy). Biweekly **tournaments** (20 matches, scored on wins and card differential) add a ladder.

Why this is the closest ancestor to Manzil's "28 authored night rules": each rule is **one sentence**, each touches a **different axis** (information, ownership, ordering, number scale, capture logic, chaining), and because they *stack* (NPC rule + regional rule), ~15 rules produce well over a hundred distinct felt experiences. Note also the two axes FFXIV uses that Manzil should steal: **who owns the rule** (opponent vs region vs random) and **when it changes** (never, daily, per match).

## 6. Singleton plus identity as a design pattern

### 6.1 What "one commander, 99 singletons, color identity" actually solved

- **Deckbuilding paralysis**: with ~27,000 Magic cards, "build a 60-card deck" is an open field with no edges. "Pick a legend; now you may only use cards of its colours; now only one of each" shrinks the space to a few thousand cards and removes the "how many copies" question entirely. Rosewater: "restrictions breed creativity" and color identity "organically limits player choices" rather than feeling arbitrary. ([Word Commander](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/word-commander-2011-06-20))
- **Self-expression**: the commander is a *character*; "people don't connect with ideas, they connect with people." Polygon's framing: a Commander deck is "the ultimate expression of a player's skill, and of their ability to use their personal collection of cards to its fullest." ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magic:_The_Gathering_Commander))
- **Every card potentially playable**: singleton means a deck needs 99 *different* cards, so the 60th-best card in a colour is in demand, not just the top 4. Combined with no rotation ("players don't rotate decks out; they tweak them and just keep adding more decks to the pile"), casual demand now sets prices: Kindred Discovery is "essentially useless in competitive Magic" yet holds value; even a common like Hare Apparent commands multi-dollar prices. Every new legend released creates demand for "some previously unassuming cards." ([MTGStocks, 28 Apr 2025](https://www.mtgstocks.com/news/17000-the-casual-commander-marketplace); [MTGPrice 2015](https://blog.mtgprice.com/?p=8686); [MTG Rocks bulk-rare spike](https://mtgrocks.com/mtg-bulk-rare-sees-absurd-2500-spike/)) Wizards' own 2020 data: Reserved List Commander staples appreciated ~45% in nine months. ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magic:_The_Gathering_Commander))
- **The hidden randomness budget**: 100 singletons means you rarely draw the same seven cards twice; Rosewater notes this adds variance "in a very subtle way" that players experience as *story* rather than *luck*.

### 6.2 Can a small game adopt it?

Yes, and the astrology skin hands you the pieces:

- **Commander = your natal Moon mansion.** Everyone has exactly one; it is personal, fixed, and *given* rather than chosen, which is stronger than Magic's "pick a legend" because it is you. It sits in a command zone (always available, returns when lost) so it personifies the deck in every game.
- **Color identity = the planets ruling your chart** (e.g. the Moon's dispositor, the mansion's ruling planet, the Ascendant ruler). Cards would carry one or more planetary glyphs the way Magic cards carry colours; your deck may only contain cards whose glyphs are a subset of your identity. This makes two players with the same 28-card pool build *structurally* different decks, and makes a "five-planet" identity rare and powerful the way five-colour commanders are.
- **Singleton is free**: with 28 cards you are already singleton. The design question becomes the *deck size* (8-12 of 27 non-commander cards?) and whether some cards are "basic lands" (unrestricted, available to everyone, e.g. the four elements/triplicities).
- **40 life equivalent**: a longer game clock (more rounds, bigger board, or best-of-three nights) so the commander matters across the game, not only in the opening.
- **Rule 0 and brackets**: publish a tiny Philosophy Document and a three-bracket power language from day one, so the social contract is explicit before there is a competitive scene to erode it.
- **Living Legend borrow**: rotate *commanders*, not cards. If a Moon mansion dominates, it "ascends" out of the constructed format for a season rather than being banned.

What a small game cannot copy: the "every card finds a home" price effect requires a large, old, secondary-market pool. For Manzil the equivalent payoff is *every card gets played*, which is a design health metric, not a financial one.

## Format architecture for Manzil

A ladder from zero-commitment to full-commitment. Every rung uses the **same 28 mansion cards**; each extracts variation from a different source: the sky (external), rules (authored), identity (asymmetry), or the pool itself (draft).

| # | Format | One defining rule | Who it is for | Variation source | Model |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | **Tonight's Sky** (daily) | The opponent's hand is the real planetary positions for tonight at your location; one of 28 authored *night rules* is in force, chosen by the Moon's mansion. | Everyone, every night, 3 minutes. The front door. | External: the actual sky. Same game is different because the world is different. | Snap's Featured Location; FFXIV regional rules; Wordle's daily |
| 1 | **Sky's Temper** (weekly rule-stack) | Night rule plus a second *weather* rule drawn from the week's major aspects (e.g. Mercury retrograde = "Order", Full Moon = "All Open", eclipse = "Reverse"). | Daily players who want a longer puzzle; streamers. | Authored: stacking 2 rules from ~28 yields ~378 pairs. | Hearthstone Tavern Brawl; FFXIV NPC rule + regional rule |
| 2 | **Ascendant** (commander constructed) | Your natal Moon mansion is your commander (always available, returns when captured); your deck is N singletons from the 27 whose planetary glyphs fit your chart's *ruling identity*. | The core community; the format people build identity around; the one with a Philosophy Document and Rule 0. | Asymmetry: 28 commanders x identity subsets; ~62M nominal decks. Social: multiplayer variant at 3-4 players with a shared "sky" rule. | Commander / EDH; FaB Living Legend for rotating dominant mansions |
| 3 | **House Rules** (bracketed casual) | Same as Ascendant, but the table agrees a bracket (1 Exhibition / 2 Core / 3 Optimized) and a short Game Changers list of mansions that are opt-in. | Playgroups; the mode that keeps casual and sharp players at the same table. | Social contract as a format. | Commander Brackets 2025 |
| 4 | **Conjunction** (cEDH / ranked) | Ascendant rules, no social contract, one fixed sky per season, best-of-three, seasonal ladder. | Competitive players; the format you balance *for*. | Skill. A pool this small will be solved; seasonal sky resets the solution. | cEDH; Snap Conquest; Pokémon's dated rotation |
| 5 | **Sealed Night** (limited) | You receive 9 random mansions (plus your natal Moon); build 5. | New players, events, anyone who hates deckbuilding. | The pool: C(28,9) ≈ 6.9M pools before building. | Sealed deck |
| 6 | **Draft the Sky** (draft) | Players alternate picking mansions from a face-up spread of the night's 12 visible mansions; the leftover cards become the "sky" opponent or the shared rule deck. | Groups of 2-4; the "purest" skill test; tournaments. | The pool plus hidden intent; a Rochester-style open draft fits a 28-card game better than booster draft. | Rochester Draft; Cube; FFXIV tournament Draft rule |
| 7 | **The Year** (campaign / PvE) | Play 28 consecutive nights with one natal commander; each night's rule is authored; relics accrue (Path-of-Champions style). | Solo players; the monetisable spine. | Progression plus the real sky. | Runeterra Path of Champions |

Design principles the research supports:
1. **Every rule is one sentence** (FFXIV, Pauper, Commander). If a night rule needs a paragraph, cut it.
2. **Vary on different axes** (information, ownership, order, number scale, capture logic, chaining), not six flavours of the same axis.
3. **Rotate identity before rotating cards** (FaB). With 28 cards you cannot afford to ban any; you can afford to retire a dominant Moon for a season.
4. **Make the daily the front door and the commander format the home** (Snap and Runeterra for the first; Commander for the second).
5. **Publish the social contract before the competitive scene exists** (Commander's 2017 Philosophy Document; the 2024 collapse shows what happens when prices arrive before governance).
6. **Count your permutations honestly**: 28 cards is ~10^5 hands, not 10^32; the variation has to come from rules, sky and asymmetry, and the research says those are the levers players actually love anyway.
