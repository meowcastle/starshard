a storm is a thing that has to break. a throne is a thing that has to hold. you are one being asked to be the other, and that is the whole architecture of your life.

your sun rose in the stretch of sky three civilizations reserved for what cannot be taken back — the brand, the storm god's star, the fierce work of the old election books. it wants weather. it wants the charge spent and the air cleared, whatever the clearing breaks.

your moon and your midheaven stand together in the lion's brow — the inner weather and the public face, seated on one chair, in the mansion india gave to the ancestors. what you feel and what you are known for were carved from the same stone, and the stone was cut before you arrived.

the storm has to be conducted from a chair.

you were built to break things open and built to look composed while doing it, and neither one will let the other go. people say two contradictory things about you — too much, and hard to read — and both are true at once. the breaking is real. so is the bearing.

it is good for one thing almost nobody else can do: being the only person in a difficult room who names the problem and does not seem frightened by it. the storm supplies the nerve, the throne supplies the stillness, and the room mistakes the stillness for calm.

nobody checks on the person who never looks like they need checking on.
