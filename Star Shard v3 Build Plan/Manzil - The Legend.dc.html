the legend

the story a new player is told before their first night. it stands on a real sixth-century legend and bridges into fiction exactly once. the seam is marked at the bottom of this sheet.

the intro · as the player reads it

fourteen centuries ago, two courts played for the honor of the world.

from india came a game carried like a riddle: sixty-four houses, an army on each side, and a letter daring the court of persia to read its logic or pay tribute. the persian sage bozorgmehr read it in three days. then he answered in kind. he built a board on the year itself: thirty pieces for the nights of the month, dice for the turning of day and night, and sent it back. the indian court could not read it.

chess was the mind at war. nard was fortune, thrown. the two courts named them the two games, and the world has played them ever since.

the legend keeps a quieter page. a third board came to both courts unsigned, from the star-readers whose art was falling out of favor. it was built on the moon's own road: the twenty-eight mansions, one crossed each night. it flattered no king. its board was the actual sky, so no court could own it, and a game no court can own is a game every court buries. the star-readers called each night's house by its old name. manzil.

the sky kept playing without us. tonight the moon stands in one of the twenty-eight, same as it did then. the board was never lost. it was only above the roof.

the history it stands on
the riddle from india. a middle persian text from the reign of khosrow i (sixth century), the wizarishn i chatrang, tells of an indian king sending chess to the persian court as a challenge of wits, with tribute riding on the answer.
the answer of nard. in the same text, the sage wuzurgmihr (bozorgmehr) deciphers chess in three days, then invents nard, the ancestor of backgammon, and returns the challenge. the indian court cannot solve it. persia declares the win.
the board built on the sky. the legend itself makes nard cosmological: the board the year, thirty pieces the nights of the month, the dice the turn of day and night. designing a game on celestial rhythm is not our invention. it is the oldest move in the story.
the two princes. ferdowsi's shahnameh (eleventh century) adds the deeper origin: gav and talhand, rival princes of india, whose war ends with one dead on the field, and chess built to show the grieving queen how a king can fall with no hand on him. the princes belong to chess's own origin story, not to the persia match.
the word. manzil is the arabic word for a lunar mansion (manazil al-qamar), the 28-house lunar zodiac read across the islamic, indian, chinese, and later european traditions. the game's name is the astrology's own term.
the seam · history vs ours
attested legend. the chess challenge to khosrow i's court, bozorgmehr's answer of nard, nard's cosmological design, the gav and talhand princes, the manazil themselves.
ours. the third board, its unsigned arrival, the courts burying it, and its recovery. pure fiction, and the intro should never claim otherwise if a player asks. the honest frame: the two games are legend written down in the sixth century. the third is the one we wrote.
why this bridge holds. the fiction asks for nothing history refuses. games really did travel between these courts as challenges. nard really was designed on the sky's rhythm. the manazil really were read in that world and really did fall out of western use. the only invented fact is that someone made them a game. now someone has.
voice notes for the surface
where it lives. the first-open moment of the game layer, before the first night. one paced read, skippable, never repeated. ceremony scales inversely with frequency.
length. the intro above runs about 210 words. five beats, one screen each if paced, or a single scroll.
the last line is the hook. it lands on tonight's real mansion. the intro should end by naming it: the moon stands in the heart tonight. then the door opens.
