the reading

how the board says what it knows. written from the built prototype, not the source: five faults, four moves, and what is not on screen that should be.

the principle

a minimalist game does not have less information. it has the same information, carried by the pieces instead of by panels beside them. every block on this board is a place where the board failed to say something itself. the work is not to restyle the blocks. it is to move what they hold onto the thing they describe.

five faults

1
the description covers its subject
hover the crown and a dark rounded panel opens at the centre of the board, over the card you are reading. it is card shaped, it sits in the same place whatever you touched, and its prose is set to one unbreaking line. you lose the board to learn about one piece of it.
2
the road does not read as nine
empty stations are faint glyphs strung on a wide arc. a lodged card is a full rectangle. the two never sit in one rhythm, so the row you are competing over never appears as a row. adjacency is the only combat rule in the game and nothing on screen draws it.
3
the count sits in a corner
"1 · 2" lives top right beside five board pips. two different countables, one glance apart, neither labelled. the count is a fact about the road: who holds how much of it. it should be read off the road, and the pips should not share its eye.
4
the hand reads as five different games
one card shows a name. one shows "two-faced". one shows "+1 while behind". one shows nothing but art. the familiarity law is sound: faces should shed information as they level. what is missing is the constant, the one thing in the same place on every face at every level, so that shedding reads as growth rather than as four unrelated card designs dealt together.
5
the decision hides its own stakes
the throne's question is the best writing in the build: the board dims, and it asks in plain words which number faces which way. then it dims away the neighbours you are choosing against. you are asked to orient 6 and 9 with nothing to orient toward.

what is not there

the board is quiet in the wrong places as well as loud in the wrong places.
the consequence
nothing shows what a placement will take until it has taken it. in a capture game the whole decision is the comparison, and the comparison is currently made in the player's head from numbers on two cards that are far apart on screen.
her hand
four black rectangles. closed is correct and it is the point of her. but a closed hand should still say how many are left and that they are planets, because both are things a player at a table would know.
dominion
a card standing on its own mansion counts twice, and spotting it is meant to be skill. a faint glow is not a spotting problem, it is an eyesight problem. the tell should be legible and still unlabelled.
the header
five facts run into one line: manzil, for the flock, the walkers' road, rung 1 of 5, imra. every one is true and only two are needed while a board is live.

four moves

the description attaches to the card
no centred panel. touch a card and it lifts a little; its text sets beside it, in the open, board still lit. one column, ragged right, at reading size. the panel becomes a margin note on the piece rather than a window in front of the game. this also solves the level problem: a card that has shed its printed move still carries the move, one gesture away, exactly where the move used to be printed.
the road holds the count
nine stations in one rhythm, occupied or not, so the row is a row. holding is shown on the ground under each card, not only in the card's colour, and the two runs of ground are then countable by eye without arithmetic. the corner keeps the board pips and nothing else.
the face keeps one constant
the two numbers, in the same corners, at every level, are the thing that never moves. name, move and art arrange themselves around that constant and drop away in order as the card is walked. shedding then reads as a card getting quieter, which is the intent, instead of as a different card.
the question keeps its context
the throne's dim-and-ask is the pattern for every choice the game makes you make: ascent picks, the stone, the walkers. keep the plain sentence. do not dim what the sentence is about. the neighbours stay lit, and the two answers sit near the ground they decide.

open

the consequence preview. showing what a placement takes is the largest single legibility gain available and the largest risk to difficulty: it does part of the reading for the player. a call to make deliberately, not by default.
the arc. the road curves because the sky curves. a straight row would read better and mean less. worth trying both before choosing.
the log line. one transient sentence under the board carries every event. it may be the right amount of narration or it may be where the resolution should be shown rather than told.
